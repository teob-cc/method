## Phase I · Outside
*who is out there, and what does that cost*

### 01 · Outside

Characterise every entity outside the system boundary. There are two kinds and both are non-negotiable inputs: **demand** — who needs something from the system — and **supply** — the organisation that has to build, pay for and run it. Nothing inside is designed yet, and no technology is named.

**Asks** Who calls this, at what volume, and what can they tolerate · and what is this organisation actually able to operate, host and afford?

**Capture the language of the outside.** Start the [scoped vocabulary table](#naming) for concepts whose ambiguity could change a client promise. Record the preferred terms, aliases, distinctions and sources beside the client table; mark unresolved meanings and their decision owner. Dependency terminology is mapped at the boundary, not silently treated as equivalent.

#### 1 · Demand — who needs something from it

- **Identity and origin**: first-party apps, partner systems, internal services, machines, scheduled jobs. Human-driven and machine-driven traffic have different shapes and deserve different classes.
- **Trust zone**: public internet, partner extranet, internal network, regulated enclave. Delegated authority (a service acting on a user's behalf) is a distinct case — name it now or retrofit it painfully later.
- **Volume and shape**: read/write mix, average and peak rates, burst profile — diurnal, nightly batch, event-driven spikes, retry storms after an outage. Predictable bursts are a design input; unpredictable ones are a capacity risk.
- **Tolerances — five, one line each.** Four are recorded per class: latency per operation as a percentile, ordering scoped to a key, durability expectations, and **accuracy** — how wrong an answer may be before it stops being an answer. Accuracy is a first-class tolerance and the one most often left unstated: a rate limiter that tolerates ±20 % counter drift and a ledger that tolerates none are different systems, and the difference is worth more than any technology choice made later.
- **The views this class reads, each with its own freshness bound** — the fifth tolerance, and the one that is never recorded per class. Not one staleness figure per client — one per view. A buyer tolerates a five-minute-old recommendation and a two-second-old basket, and flattening those into “this client needs fresh data” is how every read ends up served from the strict store. The bound belongs to the client because only the client knows what a stale answer costs them; the data step decides what that bound *implies*, and cannot decide the bound itself.
- **What this class is still handed when a dependency is down.** There are six client-visible outcomes, in rising severity — `correct but slower` · `correct but less` · `stale but complete` · `pending but signalled` · `absent but signalled` · `wrong`, which is never permitted. Say which of the five this class accepts, how often, and at what cost to them. This is a promise, and it is made here for the same reason every other promise is: **the client is the only party who can make it.** The failure step works out what delivering it costs; it does not get to renegotiate it.
- **Stress the list before leaving the step.** The classes and tolerances above are elicited; some of what a class needs, nobody in the room can articulate. Take the nouns just written — each class, each view, each tolerance — and ask, without probability and with the ridiculous allowed, *what hits this?* A discovered rung joins the ladder under the same signing rules as a declared one, and a mechanism worth designing but not building yet is recorded as an **option** — designed, primed, unbuilt, with the trigger that would build it written beside it. The full pass, with its instrument, is at step 07; this is its cheapest slice, taken while the people who can sign are still in the room.
- **Every tolerance names the conditions it holds under.** “p99 200 ms” and “p99 200 ms *during the nightly partner window*” are different requirements, and only the second can be tested. State the environment — normal load, peak, degraded dependency, ongoing failover — or the number will be quoted back at you in the one condition you did not mean.
- **Devices and networks**: lossy mobile links imply client retries, so the write path needs idempotency; offline-capable clients imply conflict resolution; server-to-server callers imply high concurrency and thin payloads.
- **Push is a client relationship too.** Webhooks, websockets, and notification streams are surfaces with the direction reversed — they carry their own signing, redelivery, and ordering story, and a partner awaiting a webhook is a client class like any other.
- **Geography and regime**: where requests originate, where the people are, where the data is legally allowed to live, and which regulatory framework applies to which class of them.
- **Adversaries are clients too.** They sit outside the boundary with intent and no quota agreement: scrapers, credential-stuffers, fraudulent counterparties, abusive tenants, and the insider with legitimate access. Give them classes alongside the honest ones — volume, entry surface, and what they want. A system characterised only by its well-behaved callers is designed for a world that doesn't exist.
- **Your dependencies are client relationships reversed.** The services you call — payment processors, identity providers, notification gateways, the object store — sit outside the boundary too, and they hand you a quota rather than accept one. Record what each grants you: rate limit, latency contract, cost per call, trust zone, and the availability figure it contributes. That last one is what makes an availability claim a multiplication instead of an adjective.

| Client class | Peak load | Latency | Views read · freshness bound | Accepts when degraded | Trust | Shed order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumer app | 8k rps | p99 200 ms | own orders: own writes at once · listing: 60 s | slower · stale, marked | public + user token | last |
| Partner batch | 50× nightly burst | minutes | export: hours | pending · never partial | extranet + mTLS | first |
| Internal service | 2k rps | p99 20 ms | lookups: seconds | slower · absent, signalled | intranet | middle |

#### 2 · Supply — what this organisation can actually run

The system does not choose its own budget, its own staff, or its own datacentre. Those arrive from outside exactly as a client's p99 does, and they constrain the design just as hard. Recording them here — rather than discovering them at deployment — is what stops the cost conversation from happening after the stores are chosen.

- **Operating capability.** What this team can run well at 3 a.m., not what it can spell. Available skills, on-call depth, the existing fleet, and operational maturity for each candidate technology class. A store nobody can debug under load is a liability priced as an asset.
- **Hosting posture**: cloud, on-premise, hybrid, or sovereign-region. This is a constraint, not a preference, and it decides which managed services are even on the table. A hybrid mandate means some stores cannot be the obvious one.
- **Contracts and commitments already held.** An existing CDN deal, a cold-storage tier already paid for, a committed-spend agreement, a licensed database. These are the sharpest input on this list and the most commonly ignored: **a capability you already own changes which store is cheapest, and therefore which store is correct.** The archival tier is half-decided before step 04 opens.
- **Cost envelope**, stated as a ceiling and a unit — per request, per tenant, or per month. Say which line it defends: infrastructure, egress, licence, or headcount. A design that cannot state its cost per request has not finished this step, and one that discovers cost in production never started it. **Record loss lines beside the cost lines**: what one unit of violation costs the business — an over-delivered impression nobody can bill, a refunded booking, an SLA credit, an hour of a paying class on rung 5. Each is a figure with a provenance label, usually assumed and so a risk-register entry, and it is what the degradation ladder and the enforcement ladder are priced against at steps 03 and 07.
- **Team topology.** How many teams, owning what, with which release cadence. This is the input the boundary decision consumes at step 04 — services are cut where ownership is cut, and a boundary that crosses no organisational line is usually a deployment unit pretending to be an architecture.
- **Expected lifecycle — how long this lives and what it becomes.** This is the input that shapes the implementation stack and the delivery machinery, and it is a different question from scale. Four cases, and the honest answer is usually known on day one: 
· **Spike** — exists to answer a question, then dies. One deployable, no seams, no migration path, and only enough instrumentation to trust the answer. Ceremony here is pure waste. 
· **One-off product** — bounded scope, real users, no ambition to become a platform. Boring technology, minimal delivery machinery, but genuine operations, because people depend on it. Do not build for an evolution you have decided will not happen. 
· **Prototype becoming a platform** — the expensive case, and the one that is wrong in both directions. Its answer is **seams now, runtime splits later**: module boundaries with real interfaces where the future boundaries will fall, and no network hops until a force buys them. A seam is cheap to insert and cheap to keep; a premature distributed system is neither. 
· **Established platform** — several teams, independent release cadence, external consumers. Now boundaries earn their network hops and delivery needs the full machinery: progressive rollout, contract versioning, deprecation windows.
- **The lifecycle guess is asymmetric, so label it.** Building platform ceremony for a spike wastes weeks; building a spike that becomes a platform with no seams costs a rewrite. Record the answer with a provenance label like any other figure, and write down what would falsify it — the second customer, the second team, the first external consumer. That trigger is what reopens step 04.
- **Consumers and retained versions.** Name who can defer an upgrade, including owned mobile apps on old devices. Published interfaces need a version and deprecation contract; rolling deployments and retained messages need compatibility windows too. Repository topology is a separate access, ownership and tooling decision. A source commit does not upgrade all consumers at once.
- **Compliance and procurement reality**: what can be bought this quarter, what needs a review board, what data may leave the building. A design that assumes frictionless procurement has an implementation date it will not meet.
- **Accelerated compute, where the system uses any.** If there is a model anywhere in the graph, **how you source the compute is an envelope decision, not an implementation detail** — it fixes the cost shape, the elasticity ceiling, the data posture and the capability required, and all four constrain step 04 rather than step 08. The three sources differ in what you are actually buying, and the table below is the deliverable.

| Operating constraint | Stated as | Constrains |
|---|---|---|
| Team can run relational + one KV well; no stream expertise | capability | store family at 04, runtime at 08 |
| Hybrid: regulated data on-prem, rest in one cloud region | posture | stores and placement, 04 and 08 |
| CDN and cold-archive contracts already held | commitment | derived stores and lifecycle tier at 04 |
| ≤ $0.002/request infra, egress the watched line | envelope | sizing pass at 04, attribution at 09 |
| One oversold seat costs its refund and the handling; an hour of the partner class on rung 5 costs an SLA credit | loss line assumed | enforcement rung at 03, ladder pricing at 07, beside spend at 09 |
| Three teams, fortnightly release train | topology | boundaries at 04, ownership at 09 |
| Prototype expected to become a platform within a year | lifecycle assumed | seams at 04, stack and delivery at 08–09 |
| Two customer-held SDKs; upgrades cannot be forced | published interface | repo topology at 04, version scheme and deprecation at 09 |
| Regulated records may not leave the building; a new vendor takes a quarter to approve | compliance & procurement | store candidates at 04, enforcement at 05 |
| Inference via vendor API; no GPU fleet, no MLOps depth | compute source | model choice and data posture at 04–05, cost at 02 |

#### Sourcing accelerated compute — three options and one honest answer

|   | API keys | Rented pods | Owned hardware |
|---|---|---|---|
| **You buy** | a capability | capacity | an asset |
| **Cost shape** | per token · purely marginal · **zero floor** | per GPU-hour · steppy · floor = what you keep running | capex plus power and people · **high floor, low margin** |
| **Elasticity** | instant, and somebody else's problem | minutes to hours, subject to their inventory | none beyond what is racked |
| **Ceiling** | their rate limit — a quota you did not negotiate | their inventory, which evaporates in a market-wide crunch | your rack, and the lead time to add to it |
| **Data posture** | **your data leaves the boundary** — a step-05 question before it is a step-04 one | your data on someone else's metal, under their operator access | never leaves |
| **Capability needed** | an HTTP client and a budget alarm | container ops, scheduling, driver and image discipline | datacentre, power, cooling, the whole driver stack, real MLOps depth |
| **Breaks when** | they deprecate the model, reprice, or rate-limit you mid-incident | a capacity crunch prices you out or empties the region | demand moves an order of magnitude in *either* direction |

- **The number that decides it is utilisation, not price per token.** Marginal pricing wins below some duty cycle and loses above it; owned hardware is the reverse, and rented pods sit between with a shorter commitment. Compute the crossover at step 02 with the real duty cycle — a GPU idling at 15 % is the most expensive way to serve anything — and record which side of it you are on. If nobody has computed the crossover, the sourcing decision was made by whoever had an account already.
- **The honest answer is usually hybrid, and the split is by workload shape rather than by preference.** **Bursty, experimental or low-volume** inference goes to an API, because its floor is zero and its elasticity is free. **Steady, high-volume** inference goes to reserved or owned capacity, because that is where marginal pricing loses. **Training and fine-tuning** are the archetypal rental: you need a hundred accelerators for three days, not three for a hundred days, and owning that shape is how idle capacity is bought. **Anything on regulated or sensitive data** stays in-house regardless of the arithmetic — that is step 05 overruling step 02, which is legitimate and should be written down as such.
- **A hybrid is two sources and therefore two failure stories.** If the design falls back from owned capacity to an API under load, that path is a channel with eight properties, a different cost per request, a different data posture, and possibly a different model — which means a different accuracy. Say so at step 07, or the fallback is an unpriced promise.
- **Vendor models are outbound dependencies**, already covered in the demand half: rate limit, latency contract, cost per call, trust zone, and the availability figure they contribute. Add two that are specific to them — **the deprecation window** for the model version you build against, and whether your prompts and outputs are retained or trained on. Both are contract terms, not engineering ones, and both have ended designs.

**Deliverable** the client constraint table — classes with quotas, tolerances, **the views they read with a freshness bound each**, **the degraded outcomes they accept**, and a shed order · a dependency list with the quotas and availability figures they grant you · and the operating envelope above, each row naming the later step it constrains · the scoped vocabulary table with unresolved meanings marked · and any **recorded options** — residues designed and primed but not built, each with a stated trigger.

**Exit condition — no figure leaves this step unconsumed.** Beside each volume, name the later step that will divide, bound or decide with it. A figure with no named consumer is a question that should not have been asked: drop it, or find the decision it was standing in for. This is cheap here and expensive later — a number collected in the first five minutes and never used reads, on the page, exactly like a number that did work. Checked again at step 02, reported at step 10.

- **Write constraints so they can be violated.** “Fast” cannot be tested; “p99 under 200 ms for the feed read, measured at the edge” can. An untestable requirement cannot justify a mechanism, which means it cannot survive the final walk. The same rule binds the supply side: “cost-conscious” is not a constraint, “under half a cent a request” is.
- **Interrogate the word “real-time.”** It means milliseconds to one person and not-tomorrow to another. The gap between those two readings is often the entire architecture.
- **Classes are the unit of everything downstream**: quotas, degradation order, isolation, and the SLOs you will eventually publish.
- **Supply constrains, it does not excuse.** The envelope explains why a design is shaped as it is; it never justifies a design that fails a client tolerance. When the two genuinely cannot both be met, that is a finding to escalate with numbers attached — not a compromise to absorb quietly.

> **Anti-pattern.** Designing for one imagined client. The batch integration nobody mentioned — the nightly partner job, the analytics exporter, the migration script — is usually the traffic that melts the system, precisely because it was never given a class.

> **Anti-pattern.** Treating cost and capability as a deployment concern. Both are inputs, and late arrival is what makes them expensive: a store chosen at step 04 and priced at step 08 has already been chosen. The organisation is outside the boundary, so it belongs in the step that characterises the outside.

### 02 · Load

Convert client behaviour into demand arithmetic — what the outside world generates, before anything internal exists. Order of magnitude only, rounding narrated aloud.

**Asks** How much work arrives, how much data accumulates, and what may it cost to serve?

Four figures describe demand: **peak request rate** per client class · **data volume and growth rate** · **working-set size** (the hot fraction that wants to live in memory) · **cost envelope**, carried in from the operating envelope at step 01 rather than invented here. Amplification, node counts, and replication overhead are deliberately absent — they are properties of a design that does not exist yet, and they are computed in the sizing pass once it does.

| Arithmetic anchors |  |
|---|---|
| a day | ≈ 10⁵ s (86 400) |
| 1M/day | ≈ 12 rps average |
| 100M/day | ≈ 1 200 rps average |
| peak factor | 2–5× average, more for events |
| 1B × 1 KB | = 1 TB |
| 1 Gbps | ≈ 125 MB/s |

| Feasibility anchors (per node, conservative) |  |
|---|---|
| in-memory KV | ~100k ops/s |
| relational, simple txns | ~5–20k tps |
| LSM store, writes | ~10–15k/s |
| log broker | 100s MB/s; plan 1–10 MB/s per partition |
| stateless service pod | ~1–5k rps |
| one key, conditional write | ~70–200/s for one unbatched, non-pipelined writer; measure the actual protocol |
| NVMe SSD | 100k–1M IOPS |
| LLM inference, one accelerator | order 10¹–10³ output tok/s — batching moves it more than the chip does |
| embedding / small encoder | order 10³–10⁴ items/s per accelerator |

**Deliverable** four demand figures, written down, each later cited by at least one component.

**Every figure elicited at step 01 is either divided here or explicitly carried to a named later step.** One still unconsumed at step 10 is a finding against step 01, not against this step — the defect was asking for it, not failing to use it.

- **Divide in both directions.** Not only “how many nodes does this need” but “does one node already do this?” If one node does, say so out loud and stop — the cheapest distributed system is the one you didn't build.
- **And in the third direction: per key.** Divide the hottest unit’s peak demand by measured throughput for the protocol that guards it. For an unbatched writer that waits for each commit before starting the next, 1 ÷ commit latency is a useful estimate: 5 ms → 200/s. Batching, pipelining, contention and the invariant’s semantics change the result. A saturated serialization point is not cured merely by adding unrelated nodes; measure candidate protocols before ruling out serial enforcement or choosing escrow. Record skew and burst duration as assumptions until measured.
- **Growth has two drivers.** Event data grows with traffic; reference data — tenants, catalogues, configuration — grows with the customer list and barely notices traffic at all. Size them separately, because a system with modest request rates can still carry a large, slowly accreting body of tenant-owned data that never expires on its own.
- **These figures do not depend on your design.** A hundred thousand users at ten requests a day is twelve per second whatever you build. That independence is precisely what lets them *constrain* the choice of stores rather than merely describe it afterwards.
- **Anchors here are for feasibility, not sizing.** They answer one question — does this need to be distributed at all? A workload of twelve writes per second against a store that handles thousands is a single node with room to spare, and saying so out loud saves the next four steps from designing a cluster nobody needs.
- **Latency arithmetic beats latency intuition**: memory reference ~100 ns, SSD read ~50 µs, same-datacentre round trip ~0.5 ms, cross-region round trip ~80–150 ms. A budget of 100 ms permits at most one cross-region hop, and on the longer routes not even that. And a fan-out does not add to the tail, it multiplies it — the arithmetic is at step 07.
- **Model workloads are counted in tokens, not requests**, and the two figures behave differently. One request is *prompt tokens in* plus *completion tokens out*, priced differently, generated at different speeds, and growing at different rates — context that creeps from two thousand to twenty thousand tokens multiplies cost and latency without changing the request rate at all. Record tokens per second in each direction, the context-length distribution, and the batch size the latency tolerance permits, because **batching is the single largest lever on accelerator throughput and it is paid for in latency**. Training is a separate demand figure with a separate shape: accelerator-hours to a checkpoint, and how often that is repeated.
- **Cost is a design input, not an outcome.** A design that arrives here without a stated cost per request has not finished step 01. Where a model is in the path this usually *is* the cost per request — inference commonly dominates the bill by an order of magnitude, so an ML design whose cost figure omits tokens has not been costed. Compute the sourcing crossover here, against the duty cycle from the operating envelope.
- **Every figure carries a provenance label** — measured, contractual, or assumed. Assumed is not shameful; unlabelled is. Anything assumed is copied into the risk register at the trace step, because traceability built on fabricated numbers is laundering, not derivation — and one that a one-way decision will rest on is measured before that decision, by the cost-to-reverse rule at step 04. A figure also has a state beside its provenance: *unconsumed*, when nothing ever divides by it, and *missing*, when the arithmetic needed it and nobody supplied it.
- **Never invent a number to fill a gap.** If the arithmetic needs an input that does not exist, the missing input is the finding: name it, show how far the conclusion moves across its plausible range, and carry on. A derivation may then propose a value, labelled assumed. A review may not — it marks the input missing and leaves the assuming to whoever owns the design.

> **Anti-pattern.** Collecting numbers and never dividing them. The most common failure in the entire method: requirements are recorded diligently, then the architecture proceeds from habit. If a component's justification cites a figure, the arithmetic must actually have been done.

## Phase II · Shape
*what the data is, where it lives, how it moves, who may see it*

### 03 · Data

Model views, acknowledgements, invariants and irreducible inputs. Retain every authoritative input needed to reconstruct the promised state; choose consistency and coordination separately from authority.

**Asks** Which views do clients read and how stale may each be · which writes must be authoritatively confirmed before the flow continues · what must never be violated regardless of any view?

**Bind the vocabulary to the data.** Extend each relevant term with its identity and scope, state or lifecycle condition, authoritative source and distinction from a projection. Write the terms used by invariants and acknowledgements consistently. Record concept relationships explicitly: association, containment and kind are different claims. The data-class and acknowledgement tables carry these meanings forward.

*Figure: Views have lineage and freshness gates. Acknowledgements and invariants determine coordination scope. Truth retains all irreducible inputs, independently of consistency.*

#### 1 · Views first

The reads and their freshness bounds were declared at step 01. A view can be a projection only when its complete inputs are retained and its serving protocol meets that bound. A permissive freshness target allows a projection; it does not prove that one exists or that its inputs are reconstructible.

Designing the read side first keeps the derivation pointing outward — clients touch views, not tables. It also produces the correct default: **a view is a projection unless something forces otherwise**, which inverts the usual habit of serving every read from the transactional store because it happens to be there.

- **Merge views that share a key and a freshness class.** A projection per query is a cost, not a design: each one adds a processor, a store, a lag metric, a rebuild path, and one unit of write amplification when the sizing pass runs.
- **Shape each view for its access pattern.** Denormalise freely — a projection has no duty to be normalised, only to be rebuildable.

#### 2 · Acknowledgements — the narrow blocking set

Now find the writes where the client cannot proceed on a maybe. The test is behavioural, not sentimental: **if this call returned “probably”, would the next screen be a lie?** Booking a seat, taking a payment, claiming a name — the user is about to be told something happened. Everything else can be accepted and completed behind them.

Then be precise about *what* is being confirmed, because the three kinds differ by an order of magnitude in cost:

| Kind | Guarantees | Costs / typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Receipt** | Durably recorded and will not be lost; an idempotency key comes back; the work completes later and the client is notified | One durable append. Orders, uploads, submissions, most “we've got it” flows |
| **Reservation** | A contended resource is held for this caller now, usually with an expiry | Needs the strict store and the invariant that guards it; contention concentrates on hot keys. Seats, inventory, unique names |
| **Completion** | The final state is reached at the moment of the response | The expensive one, and frequently unnecessary — payment capture and settlement are usually a receipt plus asynchronous completion plus a notification, which users accept when the interface says so |

> **Anti-pattern.** Treating “must be reliable” as “must be synchronous”. Reliability is durability; synchrony is a user-experience decision. Conflating them is what drags an entire write path into the strict store, and with it every scaling problem that store has. The kind chosen here is also carried forward: an acknowledgement the caller's own promise depends on makes that edge **commit-bearing** at step 04, and half the mechanics at step 07 become illegal on it.

#### 3 · Invariants — the part no view can tell you

Views describe what clients want to see; they say nothing about what must never happen. Uniqueness, non-overlap on a calendar, a balance that cannot go negative, a state machine that only moves forward, referential rules between entities. This pass is not optional after the read-first move — it is precisely the thing the read-first move cannot surface.

- **Each invariant names a scope — per entity, per tenant, or global — and one authoritative enforcer per promise it carries.** Most carry one promise; an invariant held at the bounded rung below carries two, the bound and the exact figure, and each has its own enforcer. Redundant checks are legal — a database constraint beneath an application check is defence in depth — but only when they name the authority; three components that each believe they decide means none does.
- **Global invariants are the expensive ones.** Challenge each: an invariant scoped to a single entity is a row lock, while one scoped across all entities is a distributed transaction wearing a disguise.
- **The invariant determines the transaction protocol.** A conditional write suffices when all relevant state and checks fit its atomic scope. Across several records, use serializable transactions or another demonstrated locking/constraint protocol; snapshot isolation alone can permit write skew, as with two doctors independently leaving an on-call rota. Record the store’s configured isolation. Across partitions, consider a distributed transaction, a different data placement, or a protocol that preserves the invariant. A saga is acceptable only when intermediate violations are explicitly permitted; compensation alone bounds neither their amount nor their duration.

**An invariant has a ladder of its own — the enforcement ladder.** The degradation ladder of step 01 is what a *client* is handed when the system cannot deliver; the enforcement ladder is how strictly an *invariant* is held under concurrent writers. Two ladders, never merged.

| Rung | How | Violation | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E1 · Serialised** | Every change checked at one serialisation point, at the isolation its scope sets | None | Contention and coordination; measure the enforcing protocol at the hot key |
| **E2 · Escrowed** | The quantity is split into slices held by the writers; each spends its slice locally and refills from the serialisation point | None — the bound holds exactly | Slack stranded in slices: a request may be refused while capacity sits in another writer's slice. Escrow turns overshoot into undershoot |
| **E3 · Bounded** | Writers check a lagging view; an exact reconciliation runs afterwards and compensates | A proved bound under stated admission, lag and in-flight limits; averages do not establish it | The overshoot, priced against its loss line, plus the reconciler |
| **E4 · Detected** | No check on the path; an audit finds violations and compensates | Unbounded until the audit runs | Every violation, priced — legal only where each one is cheap and reversible |

- **Take the cheapest rung whose worst-case violation, priced per unit against its loss line, fits what the signatories accept.** An invariant whose violation is the degradation ladder's sixth outcome — `wrong`, never permitted: a seat sold twice, a balance below zero — may use E1 or E2 only. The bound accepted at E3 is an **accuracy tolerance**, already first-class at step 01, and it is signed the way every tolerance is.
- **E2 can reduce coordination for divisible rights.** Allocate disjoint rights durably, debit them atomically, and fence former holders before reassigning rights. A timed-out holder may still be spending; reclaiming its slice without fencing can overspend. Price stranded capacity and refill traffic using the measured protocol. Escrow is a candidate for additive budgets, not a generic replacement for every contended invariant.
- **One enforcer per promise, not per invariant.** At E1 and E2 an invariant carries one promise. At E3 it carries two: the **bound**, enforced on the path, and the **exact figure**, enforced by the reconciler — a processor at step 06 with a deadline of its own, and the enforcer of record for anything billed. At E4 the path checks nothing, and the audit is the only enforcer. Each promise still has exactly one.
- **E3 needs a bound that remains true during failure.** For an additive quantity, a hard maximum admitted rate in quantity units times a hard maximum visibility delay can bound unseen increments only after accounting for bursts, concurrent checks, in-flight work and per-operation size. Stop or change the contract before either bound is exceeded. Reconciliation needs a completion deadline and a response when it cannot meet it. A lag alarm and average rate do not supply these limits.

**Authority and consistency answer different questions.** Inventory every irreducible input, including imports and user-maintained relationships. A projection is derived only if its complete inputs, versions and required history remain available for a rebuild. Acknowledgements and invariants decide which operations need strict coordination; they do not decide which data exists independently. Authoritative data can have eventual visibility. Name the retained inputs first, then choose durability, consistency and isolation separately for each class.

#### The seam between the two — read-your-writes

A client that happily tolerates a stale world rarely tolerates staleness of *its own* effect. The acknowledgement returns, the interface navigates to a view, the view lags, the change appears missing — and the user retries, which is how duplicate orders are born. Annotate every view with whether it must reflect the caller's own recent write, and fix it in this order: **return the resulting entity in the write response** (cheapest, and usually sufficient), then pin that caller's session to the authoritative read, and only as a last resort make the projection synchronous — which converts a cheap view into an expensive one and should be argued for, not slipped in.

#### The other seam — views a client *commits* against

Read-your-writes covers a client seeing its own effect. There is a second case, and it is the more expensive one: **a view the client makes an irreversible or contended choice from**. A seat map before a purchase, an inventory count before an order, a username field before a claim. Here staleness does not read as a stale page — **it converts directly into a broken promise**, because the interface showed something available and the commitment is then refused. The user was not shown old data; they were told something false at the moment they acted.

Mark the view a client acts on and the point where the interface promises a result. **An authoritative read removes replica lag, not the interval before a later write.** Two buyers can both read an available seat from a linearizable store; only one can reserve it. Use an **atomic conditional reservation** when selection must hold a resource, return its identifier and expiry, and make checkout conditional on that hold. Browsing may remain a projection. Before a hold is acknowledged, a clear refusal is legitimate; after it, the accepted contract governs. A stricter view alone cannot make the race impossible.

What is not legitimate is the fourth option, which is what most designs do by default: keep the projection, promise nothing about it, and let the refusal arrive as a surprise at the moment of payment. That is the case the **zero-tolerance count** at step 09 exists to catch, and a sustained non-zero there sends you back to this paragraph.

#### Then, per data class, record

- **Consistency class per category**, the weakest that satisfies the client tolerances — see the table below, and the order beneath it. The label is a promise, and each promise has a price.
- **Ordering is always scoped to a key.** Per-entity, per-session, per-partition. Global ordering should be rejected explicitly and audibly; if someone needs it, that requirement now has to be defended on its own.
- **Conflict policy, required wherever a class has more than one writer** — or accepts writes offline, which is the same thing with a delay. The consistency table below ends before this case: it says what a reader sees, not what happens when two writers disagree. Name one of last-writer-wins on a stated clock · version vectors · a convergent type · an application merge rule · or a conflict surfaced to a human. “It won't happen” is a policy too, and it is wrong; the step-01 client list already told you whether the client is offline-capable.
- **Sensitivity classification** per class — public, internal, personal, sensitive-personal, regulated — together with residency constraints and the erasure obligation attached to each. Classification is a property of the data, so it is decided here; what enforces it is decided later. Derived classes inherit the strictest classification of their inputs — and a join of two public fields can become identifying, so classification is re-checked at the projection, not assumed from the parts.
- **Minimisation.** The cheapest privacy control is the field that is never collected, and the second cheapest is the one discarded early. Ask which attributes the system genuinely needs before asking how to protect them.
- **Lifecycle to the end — and the end comes in two kinds.** *Temporal*: the data expires against a clock, through retention windows, regulatory minimums, legal holds, or a TTL. *Custodial*: the data has no natural expiry and stays valid until the entity that owns it is retired — a tenant offboarded, an account closed, a product delisted. For custodial data, “keep forever” is the correct answer, and the deletion trigger is an event elsewhere in the system rather than a clock. The liability was never retention itself; it is retention with **no owner and no exit condition** — or, just as bad, a retirement cascade that nobody has ever executed.
- **Orphans are the custodial failure mode.** When the owner is deleted and the cascade only half-completes, you are left holding personal or commercial data with no legal basis and nobody left to ask about it. Offboarding is a workflow that runs rarely, is tested less, and fails quietly; give it a named owner, a completeness check, and a rehearsal, exactly as you would a restore.
- **Deadlines run on somebody's clock.** Retention windows, erasure deadlines, TTLs, and lease expiries all assume a time source; record which one each relies on and what skew does to it.
- **Quasi-static reference data is a class of its own.** Tenant settings, pricing rules, feature flags, taxonomies, templates, permission schemes — the control plane rather than the data plane. Its profile inverts event data: written by a human a few times a month, read on nearly every request, large in aggregate because it multiplies by tenant, and growing with the customer list rather than with traffic. Three consequences are worth recording now. Staleness here is a **correctness** bug rather than a freshness inconvenience, because an unpropagated price or flag produces confident wrong answers. It wants **version history, diff, and rollback**, since a bad configuration change is among the most common causes of outages and reverting is the fastest fix. And it is usually **truth rather than projection**, so it needs its own backup and restore path instead of being rebuildable by replay.
- **Shape and size**: item size, cardinality, growth rate, and the hot-key profile — one celebrity entity can dominate the access pattern of an otherwise uniform data class. The profile is consumed, not merely recorded: the per-key division at step 02 sets each hot unit's enforcement rung above.

| Consistency class | Means | Costs | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Linearizable** | Every read sees the latest committed write | One authoritative ordering point per operation — a fenced leader, or consensus; the latency floor rises with distance | Balances, uniqueness, locks |
| **Sequential** | Every reader sees the writes in one agreed order, though not necessarily the latest | One ordering point — a leader or a totally ordered log — without a real-time read path | Coordination and configuration data read from followers |
| **Causal** | Effects are never visible before their causes | Session tokens or version tracking on the read path | Comments, collaboration, conversation-shaped data |
| **Read-your-writes** | A client always sees its own effects | Session pinning or read-from-leader for that class — and a pin names one replica, so it does not survive that replica leaving | “I edited it and it looks unchanged” |
| **Monotonic reads** | No going backwards in time | Sticky routing to a replica — void the moment membership changes, which is why a channel must answer for its far side | Feeds, timelines |
| **Eventual + bound** | Converges within a stated window | A completeness watermark, serving-time age check, and a fallback or refusal when the bound cannot be met | Search indexes, aggregates, caches |

**The menu is a ladder with a fork, and a second axis beside it.** On the order axis, linearizable sits above sequential above causal, and hanging under causal are the four session guarantees side by side: read-your-writes and monotonic reads from the table, with monotonic writes and writes-follow-reads beside them. Causal implies each of the four, and none of them implies another. Eventual with a bound is on the other axis: it limits how old a read may be and says nothing about order, so no row but linearizable sits above it — a causal or sequential store may serve a read of any age. Linearizable is the one row that gives both. **Take the weakest guarantee that satisfies the tolerance**, and where a class needs two that sit side by side — typically read-your-writes *and* monotonic reads — it needs both: the two mechanisms, or the one guarantee above them, causal, whichever is cheaper, and the record says which. Causal is not bought by default as a way of getting two session guarantees at once.

**Deliverable** the step-01 view list with its authoritative inputs, projection lineage, consistency and freshness enforcement · an acknowledgement list with its kind per entry · an invariant list, each with its scope, the isolation that scope needs beside the store's default, its enforcement rung, and one enforcer per promise · and the data-class table — truth or projection, consistency class, ordering key, lifecycle, size and growth.

> “These views tolerate a minute of staleness, so we can consider projections with retained inputs and a serving-time age gate. The hold needs an atomic reservation. Content, ownership and policy remain authoritative even where their writes do not need strict coordination.”

### 04 · Storage & transport

Choose a store for every data class and a channel for every movement between them. This is the first step at which a technology may be named — and each one must cite a label from the previous step or a figure from the one before that.

**Asks** Which store serves this access pattern at this size, and what exactly does the path between stores guarantee?

**Carry meanings into the published contract.** Name operations, payloads, results and component responsibilities from the scoped vocabulary. Distinguish requests from facts and observations from reservations; each result states what is known at its acknowledgement point. Attach primitive roles and implementation products separately. Check the names with representative consumers using concrete tasks before relying on shared understanding; record the evidence or the outstanding check beside the contract.

#### Stores — match the family to the access pattern

| Family | Fits | Breaks / costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Relational** | Transactions, joins, constraints, flexible query shapes — the default until something forces otherwise | Single-primary write ceiling; cross-shard transactions; sharding surrenders the features you chose it for |
| **Distributed SQL** | Serializable semantics with horizontal scale and geo-replication | Consensus per commit adds a round trip; operationally heavy; unjustified at modest write rates |
| **Key-value, in-memory** | Hot working set, sessions, counters, rate limits; sub-millisecond point reads | Bounded by RAM; durability is a configuration, not a given; never the source of truth |
| **Key-value, durable** | Predictable single-digit-millisecond reads at any size, keyed access only | Query shape is the key; item-size limits; cost scales with throughput provisioning |
| **Wide-column / LSM** | Very high write throughput, linear scale, per-query table design, native TTL | Ad-hoc queries; compaction and tombstone operations; secondary indexes disappoint |
| **Document** | Aggregate-shaped records read and written whole; evolving schemas | Cross-document transactions and joins; unbounded nested growth; schema debt deferred, not avoided |
| **Search index** | Text relevance, faceting, filtering over many attributes | Always a projection, never truth; near-real-time, not instant; the reindex path must be designed |
| **Columnar / analytical** | Large scans and aggregations, heavy compression, reporting | Point updates and high-rate point lookups; small frequent inserts |
| **Time-series** | Append-heavy metrics with retention and downsampling built in | High label cardinality; not a home for business entities |
| **Vector** | Nearest-neighbour search over embeddings; retrieval for a model, semantic and hybrid search | Always a projection, never truth — the embedding is derived from something, and a model change reindexes all of it. Recall is a tunable, so “correct” is a threshold not a fact; filtered search degrades sharply; memory scales with dimensions × items and the index is usually resident |
| **Log** | Ordered, replayable truth and fan-out to many consumers; compaction gives table semantics. Choosing it selects a store — the producer and consumer channels around it remain separate decisions | No random access; every consumer maintains its own view, its own offset and its own deduplication; retention × throughput is disk arithmetic you must do |
| **Object store** | Large immutable blobs, cheap and durable; archival tiers | First-byte latency in the tens of milliseconds; listing is not querying — a catalogue is required |
| **Embedded** | Per-process or per-shard local state, zero network hop | Sharing between processes; backup and replication become your responsibility |

#### Channels — eight properties you choose, one you inherit

The eight below are questions every channel must answer; several are answered *by* the first. Block the caller and there is no undelivered message left to expire, and the delivery guarantee collapses into “it returned or it did not” — two thin out at once, and saying so explicitly is the point. **Backpressure is not one of the two**, however often it is written off as one: a synchronous channel multiplexed over a bounded pool has a queue in front of it, and the pool *is* that queue. The ninth line on every edge is not a decision at all: the **latency budget** is a slice of the caller's, granted outside the system and divided across the hops — which is why it is recorded as an annotation on the edge rather than a property of the channel, and why a timeout invented locally is always wrong.

- **Sync or async** — does the caller block on the outcome, and what does it do when the answer never comes? And if it blocks, a second bit: **is the acknowledgement commit-bearing** — is the far side's answer what makes the caller's own promise true? The three kinds at step 03 decide it, and it governs which mechanics are legal at step 07. On a latency-optimised edge a breaker dropping into a fallback is the right move; on a commit-bearing one there is nothing to fall into, because a fallback is either a lost write or a double one, and the response must distinguish known durable acceptance, known refusal and an outcome not yet known.
- **Delivery guarantee** — at-most-once, at-least-once, or effectively-once, and what the receiver must do to earn it.
- **Ordering key** — the scope within which order is preserved; also the unit of hot-spot risk.
- **Far side** — who is actually there, and how this end finds out. The far side of a channel is not a box; it is a set whose membership changes while the channel is open. Name the resolver — DNS, a registry, a mesh, a static list — and then type it in this vocabulary rather than leaving it as plumbing: it is a **store**, read through a **projection**, and a projection owes a staleness bound. That bound is the interval during which this end goes on sending work to somebody who has already left, which is what makes it the number the next property is derived from.

* **Backpressure** — bound active concurrency, waiting callers and waiting time separately, then name the overflow response per class. A connection pool limits active connections, not necessarily its waiting queue. These obligations apply to synchronous calls and in-process asynchronous transfers as well as brokers.
* **Message TTL** — how long this payload remains worth executing, independently of transport retention. A synchronous price offer can expire while waiting for a connection or being processed. State business expiry, caller deadline and execution cancellation separately, including who checks expiry and whose clock is authoritative. A client timing out does not roll back the server’s effects.
* **Contract** — payload schema, versioning, and compatibility mode; on an async channel the schema is the API. Always answered: *none* requires evidence that no old consumer, retained message or persisted schema can cross the change, including rollback; a shared deployment alone does not establish that. What a published interface adds, at step 01, is a promise somebody outside can hold you to. **Replay compatibility** is part of it: a current reader must decode every writer schema retained in its replay window, or use tested migrations/upcasters. A last-version backward check does not prove all-history compatibility; choose transitive checking where that is the requirement.
* **Departure** — what becomes of work in flight when an end goes away, answered twice, because the planned departure and the other one have different answers. **Graceful is derived, not chosen**: stop advertising, wait out the far-side bound above, then finish what is in flight inside the budget this edge already inherits — drain for less than that and a planned departure is a crash with a changelog entry. **Ungraceful is paid for by properties already on this list**: an async consumer replays through its atomic effect/deduplication protocol; an async producer loses whatever was buffered and unacknowledged, which is the guarantee telling the truth; and a synchronous caller is left unable to say whether its write happened at all.

**Far side and departure are the failure mode, named.** Every earlier version of this list trailed the phrase *and a failure mode* without saying which questions it stood for; these are they. Leave them unanswered and the design has assumed a topology that is only true between deployments — one where nothing scales in, nothing is replaced, and nothing is preempted.

**A brokered channel is specified more than once.** Producing into a log and consuming from it are two channels around a store, and their properties genuinely differ — the producer's guarantee is not the consumer's, the producer's ordering key is the consumer's parallelism ceiling, and only the consumer can deduplicate. Write the eight properties once for the producer side and once for *each* consumer, because each reader chooses its own commit point, its own lag tolerance, and its own dedup key.

**And a store is brokered too.** The rule above was written for a log and holds for anything with a process boundary in front of it, which is every store you did not embed: a channel exists wherever a process boundary does, so the edge from a processor to a relational database *is* a channel and owes the eight. It is specified more than once for the same reason — the write path is a producer channel, and each reader is a consumer channel of its own. Only the carrier of the asymmetry changes. On a log it is retention, which belongs to the store, against the deduplication key, which belongs to the consumer. On a database it is **consistency offered against consistency taken**: the store offers a menu — leader or replica, the isolation levels, a replication lag — and each reader takes one line off it, along with its own timeout, its own page size and its own staleness tolerance. Name the database once and every one of those is a default. The reliable tell is a design in which the nightly report and the checkout page read the same store, and the drawing shows one arrow.

**And a store played by more than one process has channels inside it.** A replicated store is not one box. A Postgres primary is followed by its replicas; Cassandra is a ring of nodes; Kafka is a set of brokers under a controller quorum; a Pekko cluster is the application itself, spread over several processes that hold sharded state between them. Wherever two of those processes exchange data a process boundary is crossed, and the rule above applies without exception: the edge between them is a channel. Almost every such store has two. The **replication channel** carries each write from the process that took it to the ones that must hold it. The **membership channel** — gossip, a consensus quorum, a controller — tells each process who else is there. A store that moves data when its membership changes has a third: the **rebalancing channel** — streaming to a new node, a partition reassignment, a shard handoff — which carries no client write and runs exactly when the store is least able to afford it.

Somebody else wrote these channels, and their eight properties are still owed, because each one is a configuration line with a default, and a default is a decision nobody made. Read on the replication channel, the eight become the questions a replicated store is usually adopted without:

| Property | On the replication channel |
|---|---|
| **Sync or async** | Does a write acknowledge before the replicas hold it? Synchronous replication is **commit-bearing**: its round trip is spent out of the writer's latency budget, and writes stop once fewer replicas than the required acknowledgement set are reachable — Postgres's `synchronous_standby_names` with `synchronous_commit`, Kafka's `acks=all` against `min.insync.replicas`, a Cassandra consistency level |
| **Delivery guarantee** | What a replica that missed a write gets back: the log replayed, a repair that compares and converges, or nothing |
| **Ordering key** | The scope of order the replicas agree on: one log for the whole store, one per partition, or one per key decided by timestamps — which is a conflict policy, and step 03 asked for one |
| **Backpressure** | What happens to the writer when a replica falls behind: it waits, it drops the replica from the quorum and carries on, or it never knew |
| **Catch-up retention** | Transport retention, distinct from business expiry: how long the sender keeps what a lagging replica has not yet taken — the line between catching up and being rebuilt from scratch. A retention that never expires is not safe either: a replication slot nobody reads fills the primary's disk, unless `max_slot_wal_keep_size` caps it, which invalidates the slot instead |
| **Contract** | The format between versions, which is what a rolling upgrade runs on: for the length of the roll, old members and new ones replicate to each other, so the compatibility mode is answered here or the upgrade is a stop-the-world one |
| **Far side** | The membership channel's answer — which processes are members now, and how stale that list may be |
| **Departure** | What a member leaving does to the others: the rebalancing it sets off, and the writes that only it held. Gracefully, it hands its share over before it goes; ungracefully, those writes are as lost as the guarantee above allowed |

**Read the store declaration against its actual protocol.** An authoritative primary can offer linearizable reads while copying asynchronously to backups, provided those backups neither serve stale reads under that promise nor take over with missing acknowledged writes. State fencing, failover and availability assumptions. Replication timing alone does not determine the consistency offered.

**Co-location is shared fate.** Members that share a host, a rack or a zone fail together, and a replication channel between them buys nothing against the failure they share. So a store with replicas owes a placement rule written beside it: the failure domain no two members may share, and the quorum arithmetic per domain — three members over two zones lose their majority with the zone that holds two. The same holds across stores: a broker on the same node as the service it serves, or one host carrying the shards of several stores, is one failure under several names — a coupling no map shows, carried onto the ladder as one entry per promise it takes down. The rule is written here, with the store, and executed at step 08.

#### Granularity — how many runnable units, and where the seams go

**“Monolith or microservices” is not the question, and asking it is how the decision gets skipped.** Granularity is a range. One runnable unit is a legitimate point on that range — the leftmost one — and so is forty; naming the two endpoints as if they were opposing philosophies replaces a derivation with a team preference. What you actually decide is three separate things, and collapsing them into one word is the mistake:

| Axis | The decision | Set by |
|---|---|---|
| **Runtime units** | How many independently deployable processes the graph runs as | operational forces below — one unit until a force buys the next |
| **Module seams** | Where explicit internal interfaces sit *inside* a unit — no shared state across them, no reaching into internals | the domain, plus the lifecycle from step 01 |
| **Repo topology** | One repository or many, and which way dependencies are allowed to point | change atomicity vs. independent versioning — **not** the runtime |

**These are independent, and all four corners exist in production.** Monorepo with one runnable, monorepo with forty, many repos with one runnable, many repos with forty. Splitting a repository does not split a runtime and splitting a runtime does not require splitting the repository — the two get conflated constantly, and the conflation costs whichever one you didn't actually need.

**A process split adds independent failure and communication costs.** Count the edges it creates and specify each contract. Inside a process, concurrency, asynchronous queues, partial durable effects and slow work already exist. State execution model, transaction boundary and failure domain separately. One process can commit one update and crash before the next; only an actual transaction or equivalent protocol supplies atomicity.

**A clustered runnable unit is a split already taken.** Even a single deployable may contain network channels. This is explicit when a unit runs as a cluster — Pekko cluster sharding, an in-memory data grid, any library that routes a message to whichever instance holds the entity. There the instances exchange messages, state and membership across process boundaries, and those edges are channels: the entity's location is a store read through a projection, a message to a remote shard is a channel with all eight properties, and a member leaving sets off the shard handoff above. One deployable is not one process. Count the cut the clustering library made exactly as if you had made it — and cite the force that bought the cluster, because the library will not.

| Property | Inside one runnable unit | Across a runtime boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Latency** | A direct call avoids network overhead; execution, contention and queues still consume the latency budget | A distribution with a tail, a timeout sliced from someone's budget, and a retry policy |
| **Consistency** | Synchronization and transaction scope must be stated; shared memory alone supplies neither | State the protocol. Distributed transactions can provide atomicity; outboxes and sagas provide different guarantees |
| **Ordering** | Program order within a sequential execution; concurrent handlers need coordination | A scoped property you must choose, and the unit of hot-spot risk |
| **Failure** | A shared process failure domain; partial durable state remains possible | Partial failure is the normal case: one side up, one side down, and a caller who must decide what to hand its client |
| **Interface** | Checked by the compiler; changed in one commit | A versioned contract with a compatibility mode, changed across two release trains |
| **Debugging** | One stack trace | Correlated traces across hops — which is precisely why tracing becomes a deliverable at step 09 |

A split buys independence at the price of additional coordination and failure modes. Local contracts are often cheaper to implement, but are not free guarantees. Name the force that justifies the split and record the protocols needed on both sides.

##### What buys another runtime unit

Each split must cite one of these, the same way a component must cite a constraint. **Independent delivery** — it must ship on a different cadence to something it sits with. **Independent scaling** — it scales on a different axis, so co-locating it wastes the resource the other one needs. **Fault isolation** — its failure must not take the rest down, and the blast radius is worth paying for. **Trust zone** — regulated data or a different adversary. **Ownership** — a different team's pager. **Runtime mismatch** — a latency floor, a GPU, a forced language.

##### What each one costs

A build pipeline, a deployment, a rollout and rollback path, a monitoring surface, an on-call story, a versioned contract with a compatibility mode, and **a network hop that is now a channel with properties that must now hold across independent processes**. Every one of those is real recurring work. This is why the default is one unit and each additional one is argued for — the cheapest service is the one you didn't split out.

- **An invariant that spans a boundary usually settles it.** Split across it and a row lock becomes a distributed transaction — the invariant list from step 03 names its enforcers, and a proposed boundary that separates an enforcer from what it enforces is answering the granularity question wrongly. Where the invariant sits, the unit stays whole.
- **More runnable units than teams is a smell.** Each unit needs an owner who is awake; if the count exceeds the topology recorded at step 01, someone owns a unit they have never operated.
- **Repo topology answers a different question: can this change land atomically?** One repository makes a cross-cutting change one commit and one review; many repositories make versioning explicit and release trains independent. Decide the direction dependencies may point, and enforce it — an upstream that imports from a downstream is the same defect whatever the repo layout. What buys the second repository, and what follows from it, is the next subsection.

> “Co-location removes a network hop. It does not remove concurrency, partial effects or transaction boundaries. Name the independence a split buys, then price its contracts.”

#### Repo topology — and the delivery machinery it buys

**Repository topology and compatibility are separate decisions.** A monorepo can publish an SDK whose users decline upgrades; an owned mobile app can remain on old devices after its source changes. Choose repositories for source access, ownership and coordination costs. Choose release units and compatibility windows for deployed consumers. One atomic source commit does not make deployment atomic. The published-interface question buys an explicit version and deprecation contract, not automatically a second repository.

Decide the following mechanisms independently, each against the constraint it actually serves:

| Mechanism | Cites | What it costs when nothing cites it |
|---|---|---|
| **A second repository** | Source-access, ownership or coordination requirements that justify separation | Cross-cutting changes require coordination; a published SDK can also live in a monorepo |
| **A version scheme somebody can decline** | A consumer who **chooses when to upgrade**. Semantic versioning states its own precondition: software using it must declare a public API, because the major number is a promise about that boundary | The major number never moves, because nobody was ever in a position to refuse, and the scheme is decoration on a build. Two neighbouring answers exist and are more often correct: **calendar versioning** where upgrades are driven by support windows, security horizons or regulatory dates — the consumer is asking *how old is this*, not *does this break me* — and, where a consumer cannot decline at all because both halves deploy together, a **content hash or commit identifier**, whose job is identity: which build, which trace, which rollback target |
| **A build system beyond the ecosystem default** | A repository where the correct incremental set can no longer be computed from the directory layout. The tools that answer this track changes to file content *and* to build commands, and run hermetically so that the answer is trustworthy; the scale they cite is six figures of source files | A second build language, a hermeticity discipline, an adapter per ecosystem and a cache to operate — often to take a four-minute build to three. **Record the ratio**, as with any other sizing claim: measured build time against the tolerance you wrote down. Near 1 and the tooling was never the binding constraint |

**A remote build cache is a store, and the method already types it.** It is a derived store with an invalidation contract — the same rule as any other cache — so a build that is not hermetic has broken that contract, and a cache with a broken invalidation contract serves confident wrong answers while every dashboard stays green. It also carries a retention policy and a cost line, and it is one of the few storage lines that grows with your own commit rate rather than with traffic.

> **Anti-pattern.** A source commit cannot update every deployed caller. Record supported versions, forced-upgrade behaviour, offline clients and the deprecation window separately from repository ownership.

> “Keep repositories together while that serves access and coordination. Version the contracts independently wherever a deployed consumer can lag behind.”

#### The sizing pass — the arithmetic that only exists now

Demand was computed before the design; **capacity can only be computed after it**, because every term below is a property of the graph you have just drawn. Run this immediately, while the choice is still cheap to reverse. Note that granularity feeds it: each split adds a hop to the read and write amplification, and cross-unit traffic becomes egress.

| Quantity | How it comes out |
|---|---|
| **Write amplification** | How many stores one client write ultimately touches: truth store, log, each projection, the search document, the cache invalidation. A single order can easily be 5×. This number exists only because you chose that many derived stores |
| **Read amplification** | How many store reads one client read causes: entities fetched, shards queried, joins resolved. Fan-out across many shards also imports their slow tails |
| **Per-store rate** | client rate × amplification × peak factor — computed per store, not for the system as a whole |
| **Node count** | required rate ÷ per-node capacity, divided again by target utilisation. Size for roughly half, never for full: a store at 90% has no room to absorb a failover, a compaction, or a Monday |
| **Stored bytes** | logical volume × replication factor × index and overhead ÷ compression. Replication factor 3 means the disk bill is three times the number computed at the load step |
| **Log retention** | write throughput × retention window = disk. Long retention is a storage decision disguised as a configuration line |
| **Partitions** | the key first: **the scope key of the invariant hardest to move**, so that its enforcer stays inside one partition. Where two invariants disagree about the key, one of them pays, and that is the finding — [the booking derivation](derivations/04-the-invariant.html)'s room-night against guest-night is the instance. Then the count: at least throughput ÷ per-partition ceiling, and at least the number of parallel consumers you intend to run — **and how the count changes later**. A partitioning you cannot change is a decision you only get to make once: state whether adding partitions preserves the ordering key's scope, how a tenant moves, how a hot key splits, and whether any of it can happen while serving. This is the same rule step 06 applies to projections, applied to the layout underneath them |
| **Accelerator count** | required tokens/s ÷ per-accelerator tokens/s at the batch size your latency tolerance permits, ÷ target utilisation. Two traps: the batch size that maximises throughput is rarely the one that meets p99, and memory usually binds before compute — weights plus the per-request KV cache times concurrency is what actually decides how many requests fit on a card |
| **Cold-start and scale-out** | time to load weights onto a fresh accelerator. Tens of seconds to minutes is normal, which means autoscaling cannot absorb a spike the way a stateless tier does — the burst is absorbed by queueing, by a smaller model, or by the API fallback, and that choice is a rung at step 07 |
| **Cross-zone traffic** | replication and cross-zone reads become egress volume, and egress is frequently the largest line on the bill — the replication channel's traffic is write volume × (members − 1), whichever zones the members sit in |
| **Cost per request** | the whole thing divided by demand, checked against the envelope recorded at step 01 |

- **Size for N−1.** Capacity that assumes every node and every zone is present is not capacity; it is a hope with a spreadsheet. And N−1 is a moment of extra work, not of rest: the survivors carry the live load *and* the rebalancing channel's traffic, which rebuilds the missing member's share while the clients wait.
- **The reversal rule.** If the sizing produces forty nodes to serve twelve transactions per second, the choice was wrong — return to the family selection rather than accepting the cluster. If it produces one node with headroom, delete the distribution and say why.
- **Record the ratio beside the citation, at the moment you choose.** Citing a constraint is not enough: write what the citing requirement demands against what the mechanism supplies, and divide — supplied over demanded, with the demand taken at its average, so that the peak can be one of the factors you name. **Near 1** is sized to the job. **Tens** is headroom and must name the factor — growth, peak, or failover. **Hundreds** means the requirement cannot discriminate the choice, and the shape came from somewhere other than this derivation; the mechanism is not wrong so much as unjustified at that size. The ratio costs one line now and a re-derivation to recover later, which is why it belongs here rather than in review.
- **And a fourth field: what the mechanism introduces.** Demanded, supplied and the ratio say why a mechanism is there; they do not say what it adds — the constraint, failure mode or debt that arrives with it. A cache introduces a stale window and a stampede on expiry; a conditional write a per-key ceiling; hedging extra load on the hedged tier; two sources of truth a reconciliation to run. Each introduced item is then cited by the mechanism that answers it — the parent-decision rule of step 10, fed at the moment of choice rather than reconstructed in review — or carried onto the degradation ladder or the risk register, or accepted in writing. An introduced cost nobody records is how a design accretes mechanisms whose citations nobody can see.
- **Record the actual cost to reverse.** Name the rollback window, preserved data, deployed consumers and migration work. A module seam can make replacement easier but does not make data migration free. Expand-and-contract preserves a way back only while the old representation and readers remain usable; destructive contraction can close it. Measure uncertain inputs before expensive irreversible commitments, or explicitly accept the uncertainty and its consequence.

**Deliverable** the first complete annotated graph — every surface enumerated (one per client class × operation group, each with a contract, a sync or async response mode, and idempotency on writes), every store chosen with a reason, every edge carrying its eight properties and the latency budget it inherits, the channels inside every store that more than one process plays, each with its placement rule — plus a sizing table per store and channel, a cost-per-request figure, and a stated headroom target — and beside each choice its ratio, what it introduces, and its cost to reverse.

- **Surfaces are enumerated first.** One per client class × operation group, before any store is discussed. A surface nobody listed is a client touching the system through a door nobody designed — and it will be found in production, not in review.
- **The cheapest store is one you already operate.** A new technology needs a justification — a mismatch the existing fleet genuinely cannot meet — not a preference. Buy-versus-build is the same rule in a suit: a managed service that meets the tolerance table beats a self-hosted one that merely flatters the team.
- **Spend the operating envelope here.** The capability, hosting posture and existing contracts recorded at step 01 are consumed at this step, not at deployment. A cold-storage tier already under contract makes the archival decision before you open the lifecycle question; a CDN already paid for changes what the cheapest derived store is; a hybrid mandate removes candidates from the family table outright. This is the difference between a design that is cheap and one that is merely cheap to draw — and if a store that ignores the envelope is genuinely the right answer, that is a finding worth escalating with the cost difference attached.
- **Boundaries are cut where ownership is cut.** The primitives type the nodes; they do not count the services. A boundary earns its place when one of the six forces above differs across it — a release cadence, a scaling axis, a blast radius, a trust zone, an owning team from the step-01 topology, or a runtime that cannot be shared. Everything else is one deployable until something forces otherwise, which is the same stop rule the arithmetic uses. A boundary that crosses no organisational line is usually a deployment unit wearing an architecture's clothes; a team that owns half a boundary will discover that at three in the morning.
- **Lifecycle sets the starting granularity, not the final one.** Read it off step 01. A **spike** is one unit with no seams — it will be deleted, and seams are for futures it does not have. A **one-off product** takes seams only where the domain already divides. An **established platform** converts the seams that ownership and scaling have already made real. The interesting case is **prototype becoming a platform**, and it has a specific answer: **one runnable unit, seams everywhere the future boundaries will fall, one repository.** Draw the seam now, buy the runtime split when a force names itself.
- **A seam only counts if it would survive being cut.** The modular-first path fails when the modules were never real, so state what makes them real and check it: no shared database tables across a seam · no reaching into another module's internals, only its declared interface · no shared mutable in-process state · the interface versioned and reviewed as if it were remote, even while it is a function call · and the dependency direction enforced by tooling rather than by good intentions. Get those and later separation is a deployment change. Miss them and “we'll split it when we need to” is a rewrite with a reassuring name.
- **Effectively-once effects need an atomic boundary.** Redelivery plus a stable key is not enough: doing the effect before recording the key duplicates it after a crash; recording the key first can lose it. Atomically claim the key, perform the effect and retain the result, or use an idempotent destination with an explicit contract. State key scope, conflicting-payload handling, concurrent claims, and retention beyond every permitted retry and replay. Broker guarantees cover their stated boundary, not an arbitrary external effect. Version guards are also needed where an old replay could overwrite newer state.
- **Avoid dual writes.** When two stores must agree, write one and derive the other — a transactional outbox drained into the log, or change capture from the truth store. Two independent writes in one request handler is a bug waiting for a network blip.
- **Coordination is designed, not assumed.** Wherever the graph needs exactly one of something — one leader per shard, one scheduler firing a job, one holder of a lock — that is a lease, and a lease is a store plus a channel with four extra declarations: **what elects the holder**, **the lease duration measured against a named clock**, **the fencing token** that stops a paused-then-resumed holder from acting on stale authority, and **the rung handed to the client during a split brain**. Say those four and a coordination service earns its place; leave them unsaid and it is furniture that happens to be hard to remove. The commonest failure is the third: a holder that lost its lease but does not know it yet, writing anyway.
- **Geography is decided here**, not at deployment. Replication topology — single-region, active-passive, active-active, per-region partitioning — changes what consistency you can honestly promise. Deployment later chooses machines; this step chose semantics.

> **Anti-pattern.** Naming a technology without binding it to an access pattern in the same sentence. “We'll add a cache” is furniture. “A read-mostly hot set of roughly ten gigabytes, invalidated on write, so an in-memory KV in front of the primary” is a decision — and one that disappears cleanly if the hot set turns out to be the whole dataset.

### 05 · Trust

Enforcement. The trust context came from the client step and the classification from the data step; this step decides what actually stops the wrong person from reading the wrong thing, and how erasure is executed. It sits after storage on purpose: enforcement mechanisms — row-level scoping, per-region stores, crypto-shredding — are properties of stores and channels, so they are chosen against a graph that exists. The classification driving them was fixed at the data step, which is what keeps the feedback loop rare.

**Asks** Who may see which data, under what authority, enforced where, provable to whom, and erasable how?

- **Identity propagation.** The caller's identity travels with the request across every hop, rather than dissolving into a trusted internal network at the first boundary. Decide whether downstream hops re-verify or trust an asserted context, and what stops that assertion from being forged.
- **Enforcement points.** For each data class, name the single surface that authorises access to it. Authorisation checked in many places drifts; checked in one place, it can be audited.
- **Sensitivity × access matrix.** Take the classification from the data step and map each level to who may read, who may write, and what is logged when they do. A small matrix beats prose, and its empty cells are the interesting part.
- **Tenancy and isolation.** Shared everything, shared store with row-level scoping, or store-per-tenant. The decision is driven by blast radius and noisy-neighbour risk as much as by compliance, and it changes the store choices made in the previous step.
- **Sending data to a model is an export, and the boundary moves.** Where inference runs outside your control, every prompt is a data-egress event and the classification from step 03 applies unchanged: if a class may not leave the building, it may not go in a prompt either — including inside a retrieved document the caller never saw. Name what may be sent, what must be redacted first, whether prompts and outputs are retained or trained on by the provider, and which enforcement point strips what. Retrieval is where this leaks: the surface authorises the user, then a retrieval step assembles context from documents the user was never entitled to, and the model faithfully reads it out. **The authorisation must run over the retrieved set, not only over the request.**
- **Prompt injection is not a model bug, it is a trust-zone error.** Any text reaching a model — user input, a fetched page, a document, a tool result — is untrusted input arriving on a path with no schema. Treat model output as untrusted too: it may not authorise, and it may not be executed or interpolated into a privileged action without a check that does not itself involve the model. Where a model can call tools, the tools carry the authorisation, not the prompt.
- **Encryption with a purpose.** In transit is table stakes; at rest protects the disposed disk; field-level protects against the operator and the leaked backup. Each layer defends a different adversary — name the adversary, or the encryption is decoration.
- **Erasure mechanism.** Crypto-shredding disposes of ciphertext only when every usable copy of its protecting keys is destroyed. Inventory plaintext derivatives, indexes, caches, exports, backups and key copies separately; give each a deletion or expiry deadline, an owner and a verification path. Rebuilding must respect erasure markers and must not resurrect deleted material. A rebuild that might happen someday is not an erasure deadline. Custodial retirement uses the same inventory on a different trigger.
- **Audit as a data class of its own**: append-only, separately retained, with its own access rules — including who may read the audit log.
- **A replica is a copy.** A replication channel into another region is an export like any other, and the residency check fails closed on it too; a replication link inside the private network is still a channel crossing between machines, and the classification decides whether it is encrypted, not the network's name.
- **Residency enforcement.** The constraint was recorded with the data classes; here it becomes a mechanism — routing, per-region stores, and a check that fails closed when a class would cross a border it may not cross.

**Deliverable** a sensitivity × access matrix, a list of enforcement points, a tenancy model, and an erasure procedure.

**How deep to go.** For an ordinary internal system this step is a few minutes of annotation. It becomes a subsystem of its own — with its own graph, its own failure analysis, and often its own team — when any of these hold: regulated personal or health data, money movement, multi-tenant isolation between competitors, user-supplied content or code, or an adversary with a funded motive. Escalate on those triggers, not by default.

> **Feedback.** FEEDBACK — residency or tenancy rules may split a data class in two, or force a store-per-tenant layout. Return to the data and storage steps and re-derive rather than patching over it.

### 06 · Derivation

Decide where every projection is computed, how it recovers, and how it is rebuilt from scratch.

**Asks** For each derived data class, what computes it, from what input, with what state, and how is it reconstructed after loss or a bug?

| Processor kind | Fits | Must declare |
|---|---|---|
| **Request-time** | Cheap transforms that need the live request context | Latency contribution; behaviour when a dependency is slow |
| **Stateful stream** | Continuous aggregation, joins, materialisation with seconds of lag | State size, recovery time, replay semantics, late-data policy |
| **Batch** | Large recomputation, expensive joins, historical corrections | Schedule, runtime, idempotent re-runs, backfill cost |
| **Model** | Ranking, recommendation, classification, scoring, moderation — anywhere the output is learned rather than computed | Training input and its lag, evaluation metric, drift bound as a monitored number, the fallback rung when it is unavailable or untrusted, and **whether its own output becomes tomorrow's training input** — a feedback loop is a derivation cycle, and an unstated one is how a system teaches itself its own mistakes |

- **Every processor is idempotent under replay**, or it is not safe to operate. Reprocessing happens after bugs, after outages, and after schema changes — this is the normal case, not the exception.
- **Merge semantics, wherever partial results are combined.** A processor that aggregates across shards, hosts or windows must state how the parts compose — and the composition is frequently not the obvious one. Top-K of top-Ks is not top-K: sum the counts first, take the heap once. Averages of averages need weights. Distinct counts do not add. If a processor merges anything, the merge rule is part of its declaration, not an implementation detail.
- **Approximation is a stated tolerance, not a shortcut.** Sketches, sampling and probabilistic structures trade accuracy for memory, and that trade is legal only against the accuracy tolerance recorded at step 01. Name the structure, the error bound, and the monitored number that shows the bound holding — an approximation whose error is never measured is just a wrong answer with better performance. Where overshoot and undershoot have different loss lines at step 01, state the bound **per side** and choose which side the error falls on — toward the cheaper error. Escrow at step 03 is the example: it moves the whole error to the undershoot side.
- **A reconciler is a processor, declared like one.** An invariant held at E3 has two enforcers, and the second is here: the reconciliation that computes the exact figure and compensates for the overshoot. It owes an input, a state, a replay behaviour and a **deadline — the time to close**, after which the exact figure is promised. Where the figure is billed, it is the enforcer of record, and the check on the path enforces only the bound.
- **Keying and parallelism.** The partitioning key determines both ordering scope and skew. A key that concentrates traffic on one entity turns a horizontally scaled processor into a single-threaded one.
- **Event time versus arrival time.** If results depend on when things happened rather than when they arrived, define the lateness bound and the policy for what arrives after it: drop, side-channel, or correct.
- **Caches are processors' outputs.** Each cache states what invalidates it — a TTL, an event, or a version bump — and the resulting staleness must match the bound promised at step 01.
- **Configuration propagation is a derivation with a convergence requirement.** Quasi-static reference data is usually pushed to every instance and held in memory, which makes the interesting question completeness rather than latency: can you tell that every instance picked up the change, and what happens to the ones that did not? Stamp the data with a version, have each instance report the version it is serving, and alarm on divergence. Watch the cold start as well — a deploy or a scale-out has every new instance fetching the full set at once, a thundering herd aimed at the one store you can least afford to lose.
- **The rebuild path is a first-class feature.** How long does a full rebuild take, does it run alongside the live view or replace it, and can the system serve reads while it runs? A projection you cannot rebuild is a second source of truth you didn't mean to create.

**Deliverable** a processor list — kind, input, output, state, recovery time, replay behaviour, rebuild duration.

## Phase III · Reality
*what happens when it breaks, where it runs, who carries the pager*

### 07 · Failure

Deliver the degraded outcomes that were promised at step 01. The ladder is not written here — it was declared by the client, in client-visible terms, before anything internal existed. This step proves each rung is actually reachable and derives the mechanics per edge that reach it. Raise this before anyone asks; a design without it is a demo.

**Asks** For each rung promised at step 01 — what has to be true internally to reach it · is it reachable on purpose, and has it ever been exercised · and what does each edge do when the far side is gone?

#### 1 · The promises, restated — declared at step 01, priced here

A timeout value is not an internal preference; it is a slice of a budget somebody outside the system granted you. That is why the failure model does not begin by asking anyone anything: **the tolerances and the accepted degraded outcomes are already on the step-01 table.** Bring them forward unchanged and price them. Six client-visible outcomes exist, in rising severity, and the sixth is never allowed — the figure below is the vocabulary those promises were written in.

*Figure: A severity spectrum of six degraded responses — correct but slower, correct but less, stale but complete, pending but signalled, absent but signalled, and wrong which is forbidden — followed by three client classes showing which responses each finds acceptable.*

- **Two dimensions, both from the client.** How *often* may it fail — that is the frequency recorded beside the accepted outcomes at the client step, and it becomes the published objective. How *badly* may it fail — that is the ladder below. A design that answers only the first has an SLO and no plan.
- **Detectability is part of tolerance.** Silent degradation is the dangerous kind: a partial export looks exactly like a complete one, and the damage lands three systems downstream a week later. Every degraded response carries a marker — a partial flag, an as-of timestamp, a degraded header — so the caller can make its own decision.
- **Pending is often the best rung.** Accept the work, signal that the result follows — by callback, poll, or notification — and return a handle and a deadline. This is the rung the receipt acknowledgement from the data step already paid for.
- **An unknown outcome is knowledge to represent, not a failure to hide.** A write can commit while its reply is lost. Idempotency makes retry safe but cannot establish the result while the receiver is unreachable. Record operation state separately from the caller’s knowledge; return an operation identifier and an honest *outcome unknown* status, with a status/reconciliation path. *Accepted, pending* requires a known durable acceptance point; *refused* requires knowledge that the operation was not accepted. Bound retries and separate the request deadline from the lifetime of durable work.
- **Design for hostile retry behaviour.** Your callers' failure handling is not under your control. Assume some retry immediately, without backoff, forever. Jittered retry-after, per-class token buckets, and shedding at the edge exist because a client stampede after recovery is what turns an incident into an outage.
- **Classes disagree, so rank them once.** The shed order recorded at the client step is that ranking. Deciding it in advance is a design act; deciding it at three in the morning is an incident.

#### 2 · The ladder — auditing what was promised

The rungs came from step 01, and they are stated in terms of the **client-visible outcome** rather than the internal action. “Recommendations fall back to popularity” is a rung; “circuit breaker opens” is a mechanism that implements one. The last rung must still be a correct, if diminished, answer — for a commerce page, that means it renders and can take money with no personalisation at all.

**Model paths have a ladder of their own, and it is unusually well-populated** — which is fortunate, because accelerators cannot scale out fast enough to absorb a spike. Downward: the full model · a smaller or quantised model, cheaper and measurably less accurate · a cached or precomputed answer · the non-model path that existed before, which for ranking is popularity and for search is keyword · then pending-with-a-handle, then absent-and-signalled. Two rules the ladder inherits: every rung that changes the model **changes the accuracy**, so it must be checked against the accuracy tolerance from step 01 rather than assumed acceptable, and a degraded answer from a smaller model is still **marked**, because a confidently wrong answer at the same latency is indistinguishable from a good one. Where a hybrid falls back from owned capacity to a vendor API, that rung also crosses a trust boundary and a price list — say both.

- **You may only drop what a class tolerates.** The rungs are earned by the answers above; nothing is optional because it is convenient to make it optional.
- **Every rung is reachable on purpose** — a flag, a breaker, a shed rule — and testable. A ladder that has never been exercised is fiction; rehearse it deliberately rather than discovering it during an incident.
- **Name the rung's cost.** Dropping personalisation costs conversion; serving stale inventory costs oversells. Rungs are business decisions wearing engineering clothes, and the ordering belongs to the people who own the metric.
- **Price every rung both ways** — what reaching it costs to build, and what each occurrence costs the business, read off the loss lines at step 01. The enforcement ladder at step 03 is priced the same way. Then set a mechanism's running cost against the expected loss it prevents: where the loss prevented is a small fraction of the mechanism's cost, the mechanism is correct and unjustified — the same verdict as a ratio in the hundreds.

#### 3 · Mechanics — per edge, derived from the two above

| Per edge, specify | Because |
|---|---|
| **Timeout**, sliced from the client's budget | A hop without a timeout inherits the patience of the slowest dependency and passes it to the user. The number comes from the budget, not from taste |
| **Retry budget** — bounded extra load, exponential backoff, jitter | Unbounded retries convert a slow dependency into an outage; jitter prevents synchronised herds |
| **Idempotency** on anything retried | Retry without idempotency is duplication with extra steps |
| **Circuit breaker + fallback** | Fail fast into a defined rung beats queueing until collapse — but only where a fallback is legal. On a **commit-bearing** edge there is no lesser answer to drop into, and the reachable rungs are pending-with-a-handle or a typed refusal |
| **Deadline propagation** | Work whose answer nobody is waiting for should stop being done |
| **Departure**, both kinds | The drain interval was derived at step 04 and is not renegotiated here. What this step owes is the other kind: who is left holding an ambiguous write, and which rung each client class is served while the far side's membership converges |

- **Blast radius.** Bulkheads per dependency so one slow downstream cannot exhaust a shared pool; isolation per client class so the batch partner cannot starve interactive users; cells so a failure takes a fraction of the users rather than all of them. The pool in that first clause is the **backpressure** property of a synchronous channel, declared at step 04 — this row and that property are one decision seen from two steps.
- **Recovery objectives, read off the earlier choices.** Asynchronous replication means a non-zero recovery point — data loss measured in seconds. If you cannot read the recovery point and recovery time directly off the storage decisions, those decisions are incomplete.
- **Failover is the replication channel's departure, paid.** Promoting a replica loses exactly the writes the old primary acknowledged and the replica never received — the window the channel's guarantee allowed, which is why the recovery point is read off it. Two primaries at once is a split brain, and the lease's four declarations at step 04 are what prevent one. Say who decides that a member is gone — a member declared dead too fast is a split brain, too slowly an outage — and which rung each client class is served for the length of the failover.
- **Tail latency arithmetic.** If calls are independent and each of N parallel calls exceeds its p-th percentile with probability 1 − p, a request that waits for all of them exceeds it with probability 1 − pN: at p99, N = 10 gives about 10% and N = 100 about 63%. Holding the request at p99 across a hundred leaves needs each leaf at about p99.99, since 0.991/100 ≈ 0.9999. Three answers, picked deliberately: **fewer leaves** · **hedged requests** — a second request sent only after the first has been outstanding past about its p95 costs about 5% more load, not double; immediate duplication is what doubles it · or **partial results**, which is rung 2 of the ladder and must have been promised at step 01, which is why the ladder comes first.
- **Check dependence and extra work.** Shared congestion makes leaf latencies correlated, so measure joint request tails rather than applying the independent formula as a guarantee. Hedging requires safe duplicate execution, a separate load budget and cancellation/cleanup semantics; a nominal p95 trigger does not cap extra load during an incident.
- **Overload behaviour.** Shed at the edge in class order, return a clear signal, protect the core. Predictable degradation under overload is worth more than being slightly faster when healthy.
- **Abuse and compromise are failure modes.** Credential theft, a leaked internal token, a malicious tenant, a scraper saturating a read path, key loss. For each: blast radius, what detects it, and the containment action — revoke, rotate, isolate the tenant, shed the class. Key loss deserves particular attention wherever erasure depends on keys, because it is indistinguishable from successful erasure.
- **Poison data and stuck work.** Dead-letter destinations, replay tooling, and a person who is expected to look — otherwise failures accumulate quietly until an audit finds them.

**The mechanics exist to implement the ladder.** If a timeout, breaker, or fallback cannot be traced to a rung a client tolerates or an invariant that must hold, it is decoration — and it will be tuned by superstition for the rest of the system's life.

**Deliverable** a **reachability proof per promised rung** — the flag, breaker or shed rule that reaches it, and when it was last exercised · a failure spec per edge · stated recovery objectives · and, for every store with replicas, the failover it runs and the rung each class is served during it. The ladder itself is carried forward from step 01, not authored here.

#### 4 · The stress pass — perturb the outside, watch what survives

Everything above prices the failures somebody predicted. This pass hunts the ones nobody did — it is the method's own robustness move, *change the inputs and ask why the design didn't change*, made adversarial and given an instrument. Adopted from residuality theory (O'Reilly, 2024), decomposed rather than swallowed: his **stressor** is any fact about the context currently unknown to you, his **attractor** is the state a system re-enters under stress — and a ladder rung is a *designed* attractor, which is why the pass lands at this step.

- **Generate stressors by stressing your own nouns** — every client class, data class, payload schema and component name on the board, then the business context (regulation, competitors, contracts, acquisition). Three rules, his: **no probability** · **everything goes in the list, however ridiculous** · **no generic stressor lists** — generic stressors produce generic patterns, and the industry has already tried that.
- **Then the incidence matrix**: stressors × components, 1/0 cells, read mechanically. High row totals are dangerous attractors. High column totals are components absorbing capability classes they were never given. **Two 1s in one row is a hyperliminal coupling** — components that fail together and are invisible until the stressor hits, which is the finding the coupling count at §05 structurally cannot see: the count reads the map at rest, the matrix reads it in motion. A column whose row-set is a subset of another's can live inside it. A zero-total column is under-stressed, not safe.
- **A stressor run has exactly three legal outputs.** A newly **stated requirement** — signed and provenance-labelled like any elicited one, because the discovered client is still a client · a **finding** — a shared fate, an unreachable rung, a capability class with no mechanism · or a **recorded option** — designed, primed, unbuilt, trigger stated. A stressor is a probe, never a requirement: it does not enter the map's first column, and it never carries a number into the arithmetic.
- **Convergence is looping** — new stressors landing on residues already held — and the check is empirical: hold out a set never used in design, count what survives before and after, and report the residual index **with its denominator**. At workshop scale it is a direction signal, not a measurement.

> **Anti-pattern.** Buying a mechanism from a stressor. The matrix total is a measurement, not a target — the same rule as the coupling count, from the other side. A split or a store justified only by a stressor row, with no promise traced, is furniture with a scary story; and where cost filters anything, it filters *implementation*, never exploration. You never build for Godzilla — you record the cheap decoupling point and the trigger, and the option costs a paragraph.

The pass has a second firing at step 10, as the audit's reverse direction — and production is where stressors actually fire, which is why a zero-tolerance count that will not stay at zero is diagnosed at §06.

> **Feedback.** FEEDBACK — this is the step that most often reopens earlier ones. A consistency promise that cannot be honoured during a partition, or a channel guarantee that evaporates on broker loss, must be corrected at the data and storage steps, not papered over here.

### 08 · Runtime & placement

Choose implementation technology, the shipped artifact, and physical location — all for decisions already made. Deliberately small: this step allocates machines and machinery, not semantics.

**Asks** What can this team run well · what exactly do we ship · and where must it physically be?

- **Runtime by organisational constraint**: the capability recorded at step 01, now spent — available skills, existing codebase alignment, operational maturity, and library support for the stores and channels already chosen. Not by preference, and not by novelty. If this step is the first time the organisation appears, the design was drawn for somebody else’s company.
- **Polyglot has a price**: every additional runtime multiplies build, deployment, security patching, and on-call knowledge. Justify each one by a genuine mismatch, such as a latency floor or an ecosystem gap.
- **The artifact is decided here, one per runnable unit.** Immutable, identified by content or commit rather than by a name that can be re-pointed, and **built once and promoted** rather than rebuilt per environment — separating build from run is what makes a release a thing you can roll back *to*. Delivery does not get to invent a runnable unit: if the pipeline emits a deployable the design never named, one of the two is wrong, and it is worth knowing which.

* **Placement executes the topology.** Active-active or active-passive follows the replication decision made earlier; regions and zones follow the residency rules; edge caching follows the read pattern and the static/dynamic split. And each replicated store's placement rule from step 04 becomes concrete: which member sits in which failure domain, and no two where one failure takes both. A scheduler left to its defaults packs for cost, and packs replicas together.
* **Sovereignty is enforced here**, but only for classes already labelled. If placement forces a change to what data may exist where, the change belongs upstream and is re-derived.
* **Environments are a placement decision, and each must name what it verifies that production cannot.** They are the most reliably uncited mechanism in the whole system: every one is a full copy of the cost, the configuration drift, the data problem and the access-control surface, and most exist because somebody needed one on a Tuesday. Parity is bought rather than free — put its price beside the answer, because a full-parity environment is frequently the second-largest line after production itself.

**Deliverable** one sentence on runtime · the artifact and how it is identified, one per runnable unit · the environment list, each naming what it verifies that production cannot · and a placement map of regions, zones, and edges annotated with which data classes may live in each and which members of each replicated store.

> **Anti-pattern.** Spending design time on language and framework debate. It is the most visible and least consequential choice on the board, and every minute it takes is stolen from the failure step, which is where designs are actually won or lost.

### 09 · Operations

Make the design observable, evolvable, and owned. A system that cannot be changed safely was not finished; it was abandoned in a working state. **Neither observability nor delivery is uniform** — the first is sized by what a violation costs the client, the second by how long the codebase is expected to live. Both answers were written down at step 01: the degradation ladder in its demand half, the lifecycle in its supply half.

**Asks** How do we know it is healthy · how badly is a client hurt when it isn't, and can we tell which rung we are serving · how do we change it without an outage, and whose phone rings?

The three questions in that line are genuinely different questions, and a step that answers only the first is monitoring rather than operations. They are also answered from different places: **almost nothing in this step is invented here.** Every deliverable below is a promotion of something an earlier step already wrote down, which is what makes it checkable — a metric that cannot name the step that demanded it is measuring what was easy.

*Figure: A derivation map. Eleven operations deliverables, each traced back to the earlier step that wrote the constraint it measures. Eight come from step 01: why each class calls becomes outcome KPIs, client tolerances become SLOs, staleness bounds become projection lag alarms, the degradation ladder becomes rung metrics, every never-wrong becomes a continuous audit, team topology becomes the ownership map, the cost envelope becomes spend attribution, and expected lifecycle sizes the release machinery. From step 03 the quasi-static class becomes configuration release discipline, from step 04 channel properties become lag and dead-letter metrics, and from step 07 deadline propagation becomes request tracing.*

THE OPERATIONS STEP IS A PROJECTION · IF NOTHING UPSTREAM DEMANDED IT, IT IS NOT AN SLO — IT IS A DASHBOARD

#### Detection is proportioned to consequence

Instrumenting everything to the same depth is how budgets are spent evenly and detection stays uniformly mediocre. The ladder from step 01 already ranks, per client class, what each degraded outcome costs — so it is also the sizing input. **The rung is a metric.** Not “is it up”, but: which rung is each client class being served right now, and how long has it been there?

- **Detection sharpens as the fall lengthens.** A class that tolerates stale-complete needs a staleness number. A class whose next rung down is one it does not tolerate needs that transition alarmed, fast, and named — that boundary is the whole point of having ranked them.
- **The forbidden rung needs detection that does not depend on the system being healthy.** A system serving wrong answers looks fine from the inside: the dashboards are green, because being wrong is not slow. That case is a **continuous audit** — a reconciliation, an invariant checked out of band, a sampled comparison against truth.
- **Count the markers the ladder already mandates.** Step 07 requires every degraded response to carry a partial flag, an as-of timestamp, or a degraded header. Those are free instrumentation: their rate, per class, *is* the rung distribution.
- **Where the ladder is shallow, so is the instrumentation.** A class with one acceptable rung and a fast recovery does not earn a dashboard. Spending detection there is the same error as spending nodes on twelve transactions per second.

| Concern | What to specify |
|---|---|
| KNOW — is it healthy — what you promised, and how you would know |  |
| **Outcome KPIs** | **The handful of business figures that say whether the system is doing its job at all.** Step 01 recorded why each client class calls; this counts whether they succeeded — checkouts started versus completed versus abandoned, holds that expired unused, messages accepted versus delivered, queries that led to a click. These catch the one failure class nothing else sees: **every component healthy and the composition broken** — a deploy that moves a button, a silently degrading third party, a config change that makes something unbuyable. None of those raises an error or moves a latency percentile. **Alert on change, not on threshold**: nobody knows the correct absolute value, but everybody knows it should not fall eleven per cent between Tuesday and Wednesday. Slice by the same dimensions as everything else — class, version, region — so that “it moved” is one query away from “which deploy” |
| **Zero-tolerance counts** | Some outcome figures are rates you optimise; others count events that **should never happen at all**, and mixing them hides the second kind inside the first. The test is whether the system **made a promise and broke it at the moment of commitment**: a seat shown as available and refused at checkout is not a slow request or a degraded one — the user was told something false, at the point of highest intent, and a conversion rate absorbs it invisibly. Count those as events, target zero, and record what each one costs: a broken promise damages perceived reliability far beyond the transaction it lost, and users who are burned at the moment of payment do not retry, they switch. **A sustained non-zero here is a design finding, not an operational one** — see the re-run trigger at step 10 |
| **SLOs** | Promoted directly from the client tolerances written in the first step, one per client class and operation. Error budgets convert reliability from an argument into arithmetic |
| **Surface metrics** | Rate, errors, and duration per surface and per client class — aggregate dashboards hide the one tenant who is having an outage |
| **Channel metrics** | Lag, queue depth, retry rate, dead-letter count — and the same for the channels inside a store: replication lag against the recovery point promised, members out of sync, membership changes per hour. Lag is the leading indicator; user complaints are the lagging one |
| **Derived-data metrics** | Staleness of every projection, measured against its promised bound — the bound from step 01 becomes a monitored number here |
| **Rung metrics** | Which rung each client class is being served, and for how long. Marker rates — partial flags, as-of ages, degraded headers — aggregated per class. A transition into a rung the class does not tolerate is a page; a transition within tolerated rungs is a number that had better be trending |
| **Alerting** | Pages fire on user-visible symptoms and error-budget burn, never on a bare metric. Every page names the client class being hurt; one that cannot name one is a ticket, not a page. **Outcome KPIs are the exception and need their own channel**: they are slow, noisy and seasonal, so a threshold page on one is nearly always wrong and a change-detector that opens an investigation is nearly always right. Paging on revenue teaches people to ignore pages |
| DIAGNOSE — why is *this one* wrong — aggregates cannot answer it |  |
| **Tracing** | Request-scoped tracing across every hop that carries a deadline — the propagation was already mandated at the failure step, so this is that decision made visible. Metrics answer “is it healthy”; only a trace answers the question on-call actually has, which is “why was *this* request slow” |
| **Audits** | For every “never wrong” promised at step 01: a continuous check that does not depend on the system being healthy. Invariant reconciled out of band, sampled against truth, alarmed at any non-zero. Green dashboards do not detect wrong answers |
| **Model operations** | Where a model serves traffic: tokens in and out per client class · accelerator utilisation, which is the number the sourcing decision was made on and the one that silently invalidates it · time-to-first-token and inter-token latency, because a single p99 hides both · rung distribution across the model ladder · and **drift measured against the evaluation set**, since a model degrades without anything failing. Version rollout is a release with an extra column: the abort metric is a quality metric, not an error rate, and quality moves slower than latency does |
| CHANGE — can we change it without an outage |  |
| **Release** | How a change reaches production, sized by the lifecycle from step 01: a spike needs a deploy, an established platform needs progressive rollout, contract versioning and a deprecation window. Specify the rollout strategy, **the metric that aborts it**, the rollback path, and when it was last rehearsed. State the ordering constraint between a schema migration and the deploy that depends on it — that ordering is where most change-induced outages live. **Nothing in this row works without fast startup and graceful shutdown**, and graceful shutdown is a channel property rather than a process one — it is the **departure** property step 04 made you declare, and its interval is derived from the far-side bound recorded there rather than chosen here. A cluster rolls one member at a time, so for the length of the roll its own channels run old against new; the compatibility mode declared for them at step 04 is what makes the roll legal |
| **Rollout strategy** | Two named answers, bought by different forces, and the question that separates them is **what you have to observe before you commit**. **Canary** — a slow roll to a subset of users — is bought when a graded signal exists and is worth waiting for; its whole value is the abort metric, so a canary without one is a slow deploy at the same price. **Blue-green** — two environments and a router switch — is bought when rollback has to be *instant*, because the ladder has no tolerated rung below the one you are on; it costs a second production footprint and makes state migration a problem you solve twice. A rolling replace is the default and cites neither. **Name the force, then the strategy** — the reverse order is how a deployment style becomes a house preference |
| **Version scheme** | Derived from **who can decline an upgrade**, which step 01 recorded under consumers you cannot rebuild for. A consumer who chooses when to upgrade is owed a compatibility promise, and **semantic versioning requires a declared public API** for exactly that reason. A consumer whose upgrade is driven by time rather than compatibility — a support window, a security horizon, a regulatory date — is better served by **calendar versioning**, because the question actually being asked is *how old is this*. A consumer who cannot decline, because both halves deploy together, needs **identity rather than a promise**: a content hash or commit identifier. Choosing the scheme before naming the consumer is how a major number ends up frozen at 1 for six years |
| **Evolution** | Schema compatibility mode per channel; rebuild-by-replay preferred over dual-write cutovers; versioning of external contracts, derived from what consumers actually depend on rather than declared at them. Three named shapes, each of which keeps the system releasable throughout — the property that makes any of them usable. **Parallel change** — expand, migrate, contract — for an incompatible interface change, and **the middle phase is the one teams skip**, which is why the contract phase never arrives and the old path lives forever: give *migrate* an owner and a date. **Branch by abstraction** for a large change inside one runnable unit — an abstraction over the current supplier, a second implementation behind it, callers moved, the old one deleted. **The strangler fig** for replacing a system that already exists — new behaviour built beside the old codebase, behaviour moved across piece by piece, the old path removed only when nothing routes to it. Replacing an existing system is designed, not improvised: shadow reads, verified backfill, rehearsed cutover |
| **Desired state and drift** | Describe desired-state storage, pull channel, reconciliation and observed running state. A sole deployment writer does not prevent drift: a partial apply, process failure or failed resource creation can diverge from the desired state. State the commit-to-live and recovery bounds, expected transitions and conflict behaviour. Measure divergence beyond those bounds and unauthorized mutation separately. Reconciliation cites a convergence or recovery obligation, not merely the possibility of a second writer. |
| **Config as a release** | Configuration is a deploy with no build step and usually no review. The quasi-static class from the data step gets the same treatment as code: versioned, diffable, revertible, and alarmed on divergence — because a bad config change is among the most common causes of outages and reverting is the fastest fix. **Environment variables are the wrong home for this class**: they suit secrets and per-deploy wiring, and they carry no history, no diff and no reviewer, which is precisely what the class needs |
| **Flags, and their four lifespans** | Treating every flag as one thing is why flag debt accumulates. **Release toggles** let an unfinished change ship; they are transient, and **the removal is part of the mechanism** — an un-removed one is a branch someone must keep working forever. **Experiment toggles** live as long as the experiment and belong to whoever reads the result. **Permissioning toggles** are long-lived and are an authorisation rule wearing a flag's clothes; type them as such and enforce them at a surface. **Ops toggles** are mostly short-lived; the kill switches that actuate a rung are the long-lived exception, and deliberately so: **they are the degradation ladder's actuators**, the switch that steps a client class down a rung. That last one closes a gap the ladder otherwise leaves open: **a rung nobody can step onto during an incident was a document, not a mechanism**. Record each flag's category and expected lifespan on the day it is created, because neither can be reconstructed afterwards |
| **Delivery metrics** | The change machinery is itself measurable, and there is a published external set — currently **five**, not the four usually quoted. Throughput: *change lead time*, committed to deployed, *deployment frequency*, and *failed deployment recovery time*. Instability: *change fail rate*, and *deployment rework rate* — the share of deployments that were unplanned and happened because of a production incident. Take the instability pair seriously; they are what stops a pipeline scoring well by shipping quickly and breaking often. Read them the way every other number here is read: against a tolerance somebody stated, not against an industry percentile |
| CARRY — who holds it, and what it costs to hold |  |
| **Ownership** | The graph partitioned into subgraphs, each with one owning team, an on-call rotation, and a runbook for its degradation ladder. The partition should match the team topology recorded at step 01; where it doesn't, one of the two is wrong and it is worth knowing which |
| **Security operations** | Key rotation schedule, access recertification, audit-log review with a named reviewer, secret and certificate expiry alarms, and a rehearsed containment procedure. Controls that are never exercised are assumptions, not controls |
| **Telemetry budget** | Retention and cardinality per signal, priced. Observability is frequently a top-three line on the bill and the only one that grows with your own instrumentation rather than with traffic. An unbounded label is a cost incident with a delay fuse |
| **Cost** | Spend attributed per data class, per client class and per environment, checked against **the envelope recorded at step 01** — the same number the sizing pass was measured against, now measured again with real traffic. Name the largest line and the one growing fastest; they are rarely the same. Egress and telemetry are the two that surprise people — and where a model is in the path, tokens are usually the largest line by an order of magnitude, so attribute them per client class or the cost story is fiction. **The loss line sits beside it**: the violations counted — over-delivery, refunds, credits, hours on a rung — times the loss per unit recorded at step 01, so the spend that prevents a loss and the loss it prevents are read on one page |

**A published name is maintained with its meaning.** Keep the vocabulary's owner and change history with the contract. For a rename, retain the mapping from the old name; for a meaning change, state what consumers must now do differently. Apply the same compatibility analysis to old clients, stored data, queued events, dashboards and runbooks. Never reuse an old event name to reinterpret retained history silently, and do not make an API break merely to remove a harmless naming inconsistency.

**Deliverable** the outcome-KPI list with its investigation triggers · an SLO table · rung metrics and audits derived from the step-01 ladder · a metrics and tracing list with a telemetry budget · a release procedure sized by the step-01 lifecycle, naming its rollout strategy, its abort metric and its last rehearsal · a version scheme naming the consumer it promises to · a flag register carrying a category and a lifespan per entry · a migration approach · an ownership map with pager assignments · and a cost attribution checked against the step-01 envelope, with its loss line beside it.

- **A non-zero violation count triggers diagnosis.** Check the requirement, observation, protocol, implementation and failure assumptions. Meeting a freshness bound does not prove a read–commit workflow correct. Repeated fixes are evidence to widen the investigation, not proof that the source-of-truth model must be wrong.
- **Rehearsal dates are part of the deliverable.** A rollback path, a restore, a failover and a containment procedure are assumptions until each has a date attached. This is the same rule the failure step applies to degradation rungs, applied to the procedures that recover from them.
- **Uniformity is the tell.** An operations plan that treats every client class alike has read neither of its inputs — it has bought the same insurance for the ledger and the recommendations carousel.

> **Anti-pattern.** Specifying dashboards and calling it observability. Rate, errors and duration tell you the system is unhealthy. They do not tell you which dependency, which tenant, or which deploy — and the deploy is the answer often enough that “what changed” deserves to be a first-class query.

## Phase IV · Proof
*is any of this justified*

### 10 · Trace

Walk every constraint to its mechanism and every mechanism back to its constraint. This is the design's proof, and it is short.

**Asks** Can each requirement name the thing that satisfies it, and can each component name the requirement that demands it?

- **The walk**: for each constraint from the first step, name the mechanism that satisfies it and the metric that verifies it. Unmapped constraint means a gap. **A figure collected at step 01 that nothing here consumes** means the question was wrong — drop the figure, or name the decision it should have informed.
- **The three dispositions, worked in order.** When a mechanism cites no constraint from outside — a client's, or a row of the operating envelope — these are a *search* and not a menu — take the first that applies:
  1. **Surface a missing requirement.** Tracing it lands on a constraint from outside that was always true and never written down. **This is the common case and the valuable one** — and note that it is frequently a *different* constraint from the one that bought the parent: a fencing token traces to the invariant the leader protects, not to the availability figure that bought the leader.
  2. **Declare it self-derived, naming the parent decision** — and the parent is now subject to this same test. Naming a parent is not a discharge; it moves the obligation up one level.
  3. **Delete** — nothing sits above it after both searches. It entered by habit, and habit is a recurring charge.
- **Read the chain, not the link.** Record its root, its depth, and the link at which it stops tracing. A chain is only as cited as its weakest link, and a chain whose first two links are properly derived and whose third introduces an unquantified premise is unanchored from the third onward. **The finding belongs at the break, and there is one of it.** A root is one of four things — demand, supply, self-derived, or *unrecorded* — and the last is not the same as uncited: the artifact does not say, and you cannot trace it. Record it as unrecorded, name who could answer, and never resolve it by inference; a guessed root reads on the page exactly like a recorded one, and it drifts toward demand. And a mechanism may carry a second citation, usually the unwritten one — ask what else now relies on it, and record both.
- **Trace meaning as well as references.** Follow the same concept from client promise through data, API or event schema, component label and observation. Resolve aliases within their declared scope; do not count two names for one mechanism as two independent mechanisms, or one shared label as proof of shared fate. Report conflicting meanings with their locations and consequences. A missing definition is unresolved, not permission to guess. Record the outcome of consumer vocabulary checks, or that they remain unperformed.
- **Count the column.** Shared mechanisms reveal dependencies worth investigating. For each failure mode, identify which promises fail, for whom and after how long. A count is a screening aid, sensitive to how requirements were divided; it is not a risk score or a reason to split a mechanism. See [§05](#disc).
- **Fire the stressors at the finished map.** The step-07 pass, run once more as the audit's reverse direction: the forward walk checks that every stated requirement holds; this checks the map under inputs nobody stated. Same three outputs — requirement · finding · recorded option — and the same guard. Where a held-out set exists, report the residual index beside the trace, with its denominator.
- **The risk register**: rank concrete failure scenarios by consequence, affected clients, exposure, duration and recoverability. Record uncertainty, cost to reverse and shared dependencies alongside that judgement. A severe confidentiality failure can outrank ten minor dashboard failures; no multiplication of row counts establishes that ordering. Each risk names the evidence or experiment that could resolve it.
- **The cutover, where something already exists.** In a running system an uncited mechanism is Chesterton's fence rather than a razor — report it as *no recorded reason, confirm before removing*, and produce an incremental path rather than a deletion. The named shape is the **strangler fig**: new behaviour built beside the old codebase, behaviour moved across a piece at a time, the old path removed only when nothing routes to it. Inside a single runnable unit the equivalent is **branch by abstraction** — an abstraction placed over the current supplier, a second implementation built behind it, callers moved, the old one deleted. Both keep the system releasable throughout, which is the property that makes them usable at all.
- **The first prototype**: what you would build first to retire the largest risk — the entry ranked first above, and before it any measurement the cost-to-reverse rule at step 04 still owes a one-way decision — usually a load test against the riskiest store or a replay of realistic traffic through the write path.
- **The subtraction test**: if the budget or the timeline halved, what comes out and what breaks? A design that cannot be cut down was never decomposed.
- **The re-run trigger.** Amend the earliest invalidated claim and re-check its dependency closure. New clients, changed scale, new invariants or repeated failures can reopen foundational decisions. There is no two-fix threshold and no unconditional list of unaffected artifacts; record why preserved claims still hold.

**Deliverable** the traceability map with a coupling count beside each mechanism, a short risk register ranked by failure consequence and evidence, with uncertainty, reversibility and shared dependencies recorded, a list of any figures left unconsumed, the stressor sheet with each row's disposition, and a first experiment.

> “Nothing on this board is taste. Delete any component and I'll name the requirement that breaks. Show me one where nothing breaks, and it is the first thing we investigate for removal.”

## Who signs which artifact

If the model is the description both halves hold, then some of what the ten steps produce is a **contract** and the rest is engineering's working notes. A method that emits them as one undifferentiated pile invites the predictable failure in both directions: the business half arguing about topology, which it cannot evaluate, and the engineering half quietly moving a promise, which it should not. So: three categories — and the boundaries are not the step boundaries, which is why this has to be said out loud rather than inferred.

| Category | Artifacts | Why it sits there |
|---|---|---|
| **The business supplies** | The operating envelope — capability, hosting posture, contracts already held, cost envelope and the loss lines beside it, team topology, expected lifecycle, the consumers you cannot rebuild for, compliance and procurement reality — jurisdictions included — and the compute source where a model is in the graph | Engineering cannot invent these and should not guess them. Arriving late is what makes them expensive: a store chosen at step 04 and priced at step 08 has already been chosen |
| **Both sign** | Client classes and quotas · every tolerance, with the conditions it holds under · the accepted degraded outcomes · the zero-tolerance counts · **the shed order** · cost per request against the envelope · retention and erasure commitments · the SLOs that will be published | Each one is a commitment or a bill. Every item is expressible without naming a technology, which is precisely what makes it checkable by someone who does not build the thing |
| **Engineering owns** | Primitive typing and the annotated graph · store families · the eight channel properties · capacity arithmetic, node counts, ratios, headroom · derivation and merge semantics · runtime and placement | The business half can neither evaluate these nor be usefully asked to. Sending them across the table is how a review becomes theatre |

**The map itself is joint column by column** — which is the tidiest evidence that the split is real. *Requirement* is signed by both. *Mechanism* is engineering's alone. *Evidence required* is signed by both again, because it is the evidence the promise holds and a promise nobody can check was never a promise. That is a better argument for putting the map at the front of a design document than calling it the proof.

> **Anti-pattern.** Filing the **shed order** under engineering. It reads like an implementation detail and it is a commercial decision in a technical costume — *which customer do we drop first.* Whoever owns the revenue owns that answer, and they should give it in daylight rather than have it inferred at three in the morning by whoever is holding the pager.

## Amendment — the living description

### The living description — amend, do not re-derive

Constraints move. A tolerance tightens, a client class appears, a volume goes up an order of magnitude, the budget halves. The instinct is to declare the model wrong and redo it, and that instinct is exactly why models rot: re-deriving is an afternoon, so nobody ever does it, and the description on the wall drifts away from the system in the room until it governs nothing again.

**Identify which claims changed.** Sometimes a new load invalidates only a capacity estimate. Sometimes a new reader needs history never retained, a new writer introduces an invariant, or a new jurisdiction changes the data model. Follow dependencies from the changed promise; preserve the parts whose assumptions still hold.

#### What each change obliges you to re-check — and what it does not

The second column is the one that saves you time — the third is the one that stops a scared team from re-opening everything.

| What changed | Re-check | Preserve only after checking dependencies |
|---|---|---|
| **A new client class appears** | Its operations, tolerances, authorization, history needs, surfaces and failure contract; then all affected data classes and invariants | Only parts with no dependency on the new promises. A new reader may require previously unrecorded history |
| **A term or its meaning changes** | Its scope notes and aliases, every affected promise, schema, API result, diagram and observation; old consumers and retained history; the mapping, migration and compatibility window. A changed client-visible meaning needs agreement from the affected parties | A spelling-only correction need not reopen capacity or topology. Preserve those decisions only after establishing that their underlying meanings and assumptions are unchanged |
| **A tolerance tightens**
staleness 60 s → 5 s | The derivation that produces the affected view, and possibly the store family under it. The alarm threshold that was set at half the old bound | Client classes, invariants, the cost envelope — until the new bound is priced, at which point the envelope is consulted, not rewritten |
| **A volume moves an order of magnitude** | The demand arithmetic, and everything that cited it: node counts, partition counts, every recorded ratio — the per-key one included, which can move an invariant along the enforcement ladder. Partition counts are the trap — most designs cannot change them without a migration | Tolerances, invariants, acknowledgement semantics. Ten times the traffic does not change what a write promises |
| **An invariant is added** | The enforcer, the serialisation point for its contended unit, the isolation its scope needs and its enforcement rung, and the acknowledgement semantics on that write path — which may have to strengthen from receipt to reservation. If its scope key disagrees with the partition key, that is a one-way decision reopened, and it is priced as one | Everything that does not touch that invariant. Invariants are local until proven otherwise |
| **The operating envelope shrinks**
budget cut · team halved · new jurisdiction | Store choices, placement, granularity, buy-versus-build. The subtraction test, run for real this time | **Client tolerances.** They do not move because you got poorer. When the envelope and the tolerances genuinely cannot both be met, that is a finding to escalate with numbers attached — not a promise to quietly lower |

> Key the procedure to the three categories above. An amendment to an **engineering-owned** artifact needs no counter-signature — swap the database, change the partition count, move the placement, and nobody outside engineering needs to be told. An amendment to a **joint-signature** artifact is void until it is signed again, because it is a promise and it has a price. Tightening a staleness bound from sixty seconds to five is not a technical adjustment; it is a new commitment with a new bill, and the person who pays should hear about it before it ships rather than in the invoice.

> **Anti-pattern.** Re-derive the affected portion whenever its assumptions or proof no longer hold, including before deployment. Repeated zero-tolerance failures warrant a diagnosis; they do not prove that truth was misplaced, and there is no two-fix threshold. Expand the scope only as the dependency trace requires. Do not lower signed promises silently.

**Write the amendment down where the original decision lives**, with the date and the claim that expired. A model whose history is a series of silent overwrites cannot be audited, and the question that gets asked in the incident review — *when did we decide that, and who agreed?* — has no answer. The amendment log stays short by construction: most changes touch one row.

## Names and meaning

The four primitives type architectural roles. The domain still needs names for the things clients ask about: a seat, a hold, a booking, a payment. Carry those meanings from the client table into the data model, surface contracts, diagrams and observations. If a word changes meaning along that path, a trace can look complete while joining different promises.

**Source:** [ANSI/NISO Z39.19-2005 (R2010), *Guidelines for the Construction, Format, and Management of Monolingual Controlled Vocabularies*](https://www.niso.org/publications/ansiniso-z3919-2005-r2010). It governs vocabulary for indexing and retrieval. The rules below adapt its term selection, scope, relationships, testing and maintenance to system descriptions; the API and compatibility consequences are TEOB's application, not requirements stated by NISO.

| Naming rule | What the design records | Source sections |
|---|---|---|
| **Use language with a source** | Choose terms from client usage, domain documents and the organisation's established language. Record which source supports a disputed or unfamiliar choice. An invented term remains a proposal until its meaning is agreed; neither a popular word nor an internal preference settles a disagreement about behaviour. | §§5.3.5, 6.6.1 |
| **Prefer one term per concept, within a scope** | State the domain in which the term holds, choose its preferred form and record aliases. Different domains may legitimately use different meanings; map them at their boundary. A shared spelling is not evidence of a shared concept, and an alias in documentation does not promise a second supported API endpoint. | §§5.3, 6.2, 8.2 |
| **Resolve ambiguity at the point of use** | Use a natural compound or an explicit namespace where meanings meet: `AccessToken`, `PageCursor`, `AdmissionPermit`. Qualify each conflicting sense. A short local name is sufficient where its scope is visible and unambiguous. | §6.2.1 |
| **Write the boundary of the meaning** | A scope note states what the term includes and distinguishes it from nearby terms. Define *accepted*, *committed* and *completed* by the condition each reports. Define *unknown* as what this observer cannot establish; it is not evidence of a pending operation or a refusal. Cross-reference both sides of a distinction. | §6.2.2 |
| **Keep meaningful compounds and recognisable forms** | Use natural word order and modifiers that narrow a concept: *seat hold* identifies more than *hold*. Keep established compounds intact. Prefer full forms unless an abbreviation is established and unambiguous for these consumers. Apply the language's identifier conventions separately; NISO's indexing rules for noun forms do not prescribe API verbs, casing or plural resource paths. | §§6.3.1, 6.6.2–6.6.4, 7.4–7.5, 7.8 |
| **Type the relationship too** | Distinguish *is a kind of*, *is an instance of*, *is part of* and *is related to*. A payment associated with a booking is not therefore a kind of booking. Vocabulary relationships alone justify neither software inheritance nor a runtime split. | §§8.3–8.4 |
| **Retain meaning through change** | For published terms, record the previous form and scope, the change date, reason, owner and replacement where one exists. Separate a label change from a meaning change. Trace effects on clients, stored schemas, retained messages, metrics and historical records before choosing the migration and compatibility window. | §§6.2.3, 11.3 |
| **Test the vocabulary on tasks** | Ask representative consumers to select an operation for a concrete task and explain what its response establishes. Record who was asked, the task, disagreements and revisions. Until performed, this is a proposed check, not validation. A naming review cites the conflicting usages or name–contract mismatch and its consequence; unfamiliar style alone is not a correctness defect. | §11.2 |

**Keep a small vocabulary table where meanings affect a promise:** preferred term · scope and meaning · aliases · distinction from nearby concepts · source. Unresolved choices stay marked *unresolved*, with who can settle them. Put this beside the affected client or contract table; do not invent a global dictionary or catalogue every local variable. Add ownership and history where publication or change makes them necessary.

> **Anti-pattern.** **Retrieval equivalence is not behavioural equivalence.** NISO §§8.2.3–8.2.4 permit near-synonyms and broader terms to stand in for narrower ones for retrieval. A documentation search may connect *cancel* and *refund*; an executable contract must retain their distinction where one stops an obligation and the other returns money. Do not merge states, operations or permissions because their names are related.

**Name the responsibility; declare the role; record the implementation separately.** *Seat availability view* is a domain responsibility played by a derived store. Its product belongs in the implementation column. A runnable component playing several primitive roles gets several role entries, not an invented fifth primitive. Different role names do not require different processes.

**Let operation and result names follow the contract.** An illustrative `RequestSeatHold` asks for a transition; `SeatHeld` reports the specified hold only after it exists. Neither spelling supplies atomicity, durability or authority. Refusal, known pending work and an outcome unknown to the caller remain distinguishable in the contract. Existing public names are assessed for consequences and changed through compatibility rules, not renamed for cosmetic uniformity.
