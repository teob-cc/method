::: def
Make the design observable, evolvable, and owned. A system that cannot be changed safely was not finished; it was abandoned in a working state. **Neither observability nor delivery is uniform** — the first is sized by what a violation costs the client, the second by how long the codebase is expected to live. Both answers were written down at step 01: the degradation ladder in its demand half, the lifecycle in its supply half.
:::

::: asks
ASKS · How do we know it is healthy · how badly is a client hurt when it isn't, and can we tell which rung we are serving · how do we change it without an outage, and whose phone rings?
:::

The three questions in that line are genuinely different questions, and a step that answers only the first is monitoring rather than operations. They are also answered from different places: **almost nothing in this step is invented here.** Every deliverable below is a promotion of something an earlier step already wrote down, which is what makes it checkable — a metric that cannot name the step that demanded it is measuring what was easy.

### Detection is proportioned to consequence {style="color:var(--blue);font-size:12.5px;margin:16px 0 4px"}

Instrumenting everything to the same depth is how budgets are spent evenly and detection stays uniformly mediocre. The ladder from step 01 already ranks, per client class, what each degraded outcome costs — so it is also the sizing input. **The rung is a metric.** Not “is it up”, but: which rung is each client class being served right now, and how long has it been there?

- **Detection sharpens as the fall lengthens.** A class that tolerates stale-complete needs a staleness number. A class whose next rung down is one it does not tolerate needs that transition alarmed, fast, and named — that boundary is the whole point of having ranked them.
- **The forbidden rung needs detection that does not depend on the system being healthy.** A system serving wrong answers looks fine from the inside: the dashboards are green, because being wrong is not slow. That case is a **continuous audit** — a reconciliation, an invariant checked out of band, a sampled comparison against truth.
- **Count the markers the ladder already mandates.** Step 07 requires every degraded response to carry a partial flag, an as-of timestamp, or a degraded header. Those are free instrumentation: their rate, per class, *is* the rung distribution.
- **Where the ladder is shallow, so is the instrumentation.** A class with one acceptable rung and a fast recovery does not earn a dashboard. Spending detection there is the same error as spending nodes on twelve transactions per second.

State the ordering constraint between a schema migration and the deploy that depends on it — that ordering is where most change-induced outages live. <b>Nothing in this row works without fast startup and graceful shutdown</b>, and graceful shutdown is a channel property rather than a process one — it is the <b>departure</b> property step <span class="num">04</span> made you declare, and its interval is derived from the far-side bound recorded there rather than chosen here. <tr><td><b>Rollout strategy</b></td><td>Two named answers, bought by different forces, and the question that separates them is <b>what you have to observe before you commit</b>. <b>Canary</b> — a slow roll to a subset of users — is bought when a graded signal exists and is worth waiting for; its whole value is the abort metric, so a canary without one is a slow deploy at the same price. <b>Blue-green</b> — two environments and a router switch — is bought when rollback has to be <em>instant</em>, because the ladder has no tolerated rung below the one you are on; it costs a second production footprint and makes state migration a problem you solve twice. A rolling replace is the default and cites neither. <b>Name the force, then the strategy</b> — the reverse order is how a deployment style becomes a house preference</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Version scheme</b></td><td>Derived from <b>who can decline an upgrade</b>, which step 01 recorded under consumers you cannot rebuild for. A consumer who chooses when to upgrade is owed a compatibility promise, and <b>semantic versioning requires a declared public API</b> for exactly that reason. A consumer whose upgrade is driven by time rather than compatibility — a support window, a security horizon, a regulatory date — is better served by <b>calendar versioning</b>, because the question actually being asked is <i>how old is this</i>. A consumer who cannot decline, because both halves deploy together, needs <b>identity rather than a promise</b>: a content hash or commit identifier. Choosing the scheme before naming the consumer is how a major number ends up frozen at 1 for six years</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Evolution</b></td><td>Schema compatibility mode per channel; rebuild-by-replay preferred over dual-write cutovers; versioning of external contracts, derived from what consumers actually depend on rather than declared at them. **A published name is maintained with its meaning.** Keep the vocabulary's owner and change history with the contract. For a rename, retain the mapping from the old name; for a meaning change, state what consumers must now do differently. Apply the same compatibility analysis to old clients, stored data, queued events, dashboards and runbooks. Never reuse an old event name to reinterpret retained history silently, and do not make an API break merely to remove a harmless naming inconsistency.

::: prod
→ PRODUCES: the outcome-KPI list with its investigation triggers · an SLO table · rung metrics and audits derived from the step-01 ladder · a metrics and tracing list with a telemetry budget · a release procedure sized by the step-01 lifecycle, naming its rollout strategy, its abort metric and its last rehearsal · a version scheme naming the consumer it promises to · a flag register carrying a category and a lifespan per entry · a migration approach · an ownership map with pager assignments · and a cost attribution checked against the step-01 envelope, with its loss line beside it.
:::

- **A non-zero violation count triggers diagnosis.** Check the requirement, observation, protocol, implementation and failure assumptions. Meeting a freshness bound does not prove a read–commit workflow correct. Repeated fixes are evidence to widen the investigation, not proof that the source-of-truth model must be wrong.
- **Rehearsal dates are part of the deliverable.** A rollback path, a restore, a failover and a containment procedure are assumptions until each has a date attached. This is the same rule the failure step applies to degradation rungs, applied to the procedures that recover from them.
- **Uniformity is the tell.** An operations plan that treats every client class alike has read neither of its inputs — it has bought the same insurance for the ledger and the recommendations carousel.

::: warn
Specifying dashboards and calling it observability. Rate, errors and duration tell you the system is unhealthy. They do not tell you which dependency, which tenant, or which deploy — and the deploy is the answer often enough that “what changed” deserves to be a first-class query.
:::
