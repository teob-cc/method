## Where the ideas come from

::: lede
The method contributes an order and a discipline — the ideas themselves have parents. Naming them is honesty, and it is also a reading list.
:::

- **Truth store plus rebuildable derived views** — Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* and “Turning the Database Inside Out”; Kreps, “The Log”.
- **SLOs promoted from client tolerances, error budgets as arithmetic** — the Google SRE workbook.
- **Context before containers** — Brown's C4 model; the system-context diagram is step 01 drawn.
- **Timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads** — Nygard, *Release It!*, 2nd ed., 2018. His stability patterns, and the ship-compartment metaphor is his. **Not his**, though the method previously implied otherwise: the *timeout budget* sliced from a caller and divided across hops is deadline propagation from the tracing and SRE literature, and the **degradation ladder has no established parent** — “graceful degradation” appears once in that book, about queueing latency. He supplies the mechanics; the ranked, client-visible rungs appear to be new here. Fowler's bliki entry on the circuit breaker is, as with Bounded Context, the version most engineers have actually read.
- **The change patterns named at step 09 and step 10** — all published on martinfowler.com, and worth attributing precisely, because three of the six are other people's and two more are his write-ups of other people's ideas. *Strangler fig application* (2004, rewritten 2024) and *branch by abstraction* (2014, his write-up crediting Paul Hammant for the name and Stacy Curl for the technique) are Fowler's; *blue-green deployment* (2010) is his write-up crediting Daniel Terhorst-North and Jez Humble for the name; **parallel change**, the expand–migrate–contract migration (2014) and **canary release** (2014) are **Danilo Sato's**; the four-category **feature-toggle taxonomy** (2016, revised 2017) — release · experiment · ops · permissioning, ranked by longevity and dynamism — is **Pete Hodgson's**, and it supplies the actuator the degradation ladder needs: an *ops* toggle is a rung made operable. Ops toggles are mostly short-lived; the kill switches that actuate a rung are the long-lived exception, and deliberately so — and long-lived is exactly what a release toggle must not be.
- **Public versus published** — Fowler, *Published Interface* (2003). A consumer outside the release boundary creates a compatibility obligation, including for asynchronous schemas. Repository separation is a different decision; a monorepo can publish several independently versioned contracts.
- **The boundary, and the entities around it** — Jackson, *The World and the Machine* (ICSE 1995): the machine furnishes the solution, but the problem is in the world. The context diagram that draws the system as one box with the external entities arranged around it is Gane & Sarson (1977) and DeMarco (1978); §02 opens with what this method renamed and what it changed.
- **Bounded Context** — Evans, *Domain-Driven Design* (2003), Part IV; Fowler's bliki entry is the version most engineers have read. A model is true only inside a declared boundary, and integration means mapping between models rather than merging them. Evans bounds meaning; this method bounds load, failure and cost — and **aggregate**, used in the worked example for the unit inside which an invariant holds and writes serialise, is his word in his sense.
- **Contracts derived from consumers** — Ian Robinson, “Consumer-Driven Contracts” (martinfowler.com, 2006). The closest prior statement of this method's direction of causality, applied to interfaces: “provider contracts emerge to meet consumer expectations and demands” — that is, “providers are subject to an obligation that originates from outside their boundaries”. That is the outside-in claim in the language of integration, twenty years early — and it is also the mechanism behind the compatibility mode every channel declares at step 04.
- **Architecture as a shared understanding** — Ralph Johnson, in a mailing-list posting Fowler quotes whole in “Who Needs an Architect?” (IEEE Software, 2003): “the expert developers working on that project have a shared understanding of the system design. This shared understanding is called ‘architecture’”, and “Architecture is about the important stuff. Whatever that is.” The claim in §01 is his, extended in two directions — across the engineering–business boundary rather than across the expert developers, and into numbers. The ten steps are an answer to the second sentence.
- **The layer immediately below this one** — Fowler, *Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture* (2002). Active Record, Data Mapper, Unit of Work, Identity Map, Repository, Service Layer, Transaction Script: each is a way of *implementing* a processor or a store. This method stops at the role and its constraints, which is the same demarcation §01 draws between the store and the Postgres that is sufficient to play it. The omission is a boundary, not an oversight — but the boundary is worth knowing, because that is the layer you drop into the moment the derivation is finished.
- **Outside-in as a named discipline** — Kessler & Sweitzer, *Outside-in Software Development* (IBM Press, 2007). Stakeholders first, then organizational context, then success defined in stakeholders' terms: step 01's two halves and step 09's outcome KPIs, nineteen years earlier and without a single number. The name and the framing are theirs.
- **Two-direction traceability, and the rule for an orphan** — the regulated industries had both long before this method. CMMI's requirements-management practice asks whether every lower-level requirement traces to a valid source; DO-178C mandates the trace in both directions so that orphan and dead code are not produced; and NASA's *Systems Engineering Handbook* (NASA/SP-2016-6105 Rev2, §6.2.1.2.3) has the rule almost word for word, with both its alternatives — a requirement with no parent that is not an acceptable self-derived one means either that the traceability process is flawed and should be redone, or that the requirement is gold plating and should be eliminated. The softer, three-way disposition at step 10 follows the form that community chose on purpose.
- **The requirement-to-mechanism matrix, the solution-neutral requirement, and the ordered pipeline of written artifacts** — QFD's House of Quality (Akao, 1966; Kobe, 1972) owns the matrix whose empty rows and columns are read as gaps and unjustified mechanisms. Suh's axiomatic design (1990) owns the formal version, the solution-neutral requirement, and a name for a design parameter with no functional requirement: *redundant design*. Pahl & Beitz and VDI 2221 own the pipeline in which each step consumes the output of the one before. All of them predate this method by decades.
- **The deletion test** — related to TRIZ trimming. Here it asks which obligations lose support when a mechanism is removed. Removing an individual requirement is not equivalent: another requirement or a failure scenario may still justify a shared component.
- **Arithmetic in two passes** — Smith & Williams, software performance engineering (2001): software resource requirements in technology-neutral units first, computer resource requirements mapped onto devices second. Operational analysis has had the visit ratio — this method's amplification factor — since Buzen (1976). What is added here is the gate — the first pass is a precondition on choosing a technology, not a modelling convenience.
- **Quality-attribute scenarios, and drivers before elements** — the SEI's ADD, ATAM and QAW. Every check there runs from driver to element; the walk back from element to driver, and the sizing arithmetic, are not in the ADD, ATAM and QAW reports read for this method — a bounded reading, not a claim about the whole SEI corpus.
- **Threads that cross every step** — Rozanski & Woods's perspectives, applied across a system's architectural views, and their Context viewpoint, which informs the scope of all the others. A catalogue to pick from, where this is an ordered pipeline.
- **The three acknowledgement kinds** — Helland, *Life beyond Distributed Transactions* (CIDR 2007): tentative, cancelling and confirming operations. A reservation is his tentative operation with an expiry, and a completion is his confirming one. The taxonomy is his; using it to scope strict coordination is step 03's. The [foreword](foreword.html#owed) sets out each of these debts in full, with what differs.
- **The order of the consistency menu** — Jepsen's map of consistency models: linearizable implies sequential, which implies causal; causal implies writes-follow-reads and PRAM, and PRAM implies monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read-your-writes. Between read-your-writes and monotonic reads the map has no arrow, which is the fork at step 03. The four session guarantees are named after Terry, Demers, Petersen, Spreitzer, Theimer & Welch, “Session Guarantees for Weakly Consistent Replicated Data” (PDIS 1994).
- **Protocol references for V4.12.** [AWS on idempotent APIs](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/making-retries-safe-with-idempotent-APIs/) explains coupling the request token with mutations; [gRPC on deadlines](https://grpc.io/blog/deadlines/) separates client and server assessments of success; [NIST’s cryptographic-erase definition](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/cryptographic_erase) applies to data protected by the destroyed key; [Confluent’s compatibility modes](https://docs.confluent.io/platform/current/schema-registry/fundamentals/schema-evolution.html) distinguish previous-version checks from transitive history checks. These support the narrow protocol points, not empirical validation of the method as a whole.
- **Isolation read off the scope, and the escrowed rung** — write skew under snapshot isolation is Berenson, Bernstein, Gray, Melton, O'Neil & O'Neil, “A Critique of ANSI SQL Isolation Levels” (SIGMOD 1995), §4.2; that PostgreSQL defaults to read committed and implements repeatable read as snapshot isolation is its own documentation, §13.2. Escrow is O'Neil, “The Escrow Transactional Method” (*ACM TODS*, 1986): concurrent updates to an aggregate quantity without the item becoming a bottleneck. The demarcation protocol of Barbará & Garcia-Molina (*The VLDB Journal*, 1994) keeps a constraint true at all times with safe limits held locally per site and changed dynamically. The enforcement ladder that orders them, and the loss line each rung is priced against, are this method's.
- **The fan-out tail and the hedged request** — Dean & Barroso, “The Tail at Scale” (*Communications of the ACM*, 2013): a request collecting from a hundred servers with a one-second p99 takes more than a second [63%]{.num} of the time, and deferring the second request until the first has been outstanding past the 95th-percentile latency limits the extra load to about [5%]{.num}.
- **Barry O'Reilly, *Residues* (2024)** — the stress pass is his, decomposed. Owed: stress-first discovery of candidate requirements earlier elicitation may have missed · residues that are business processes, not software · **hyperliminal coupling** as a named, mechanical instrument · the residue-as-option cost discipline (design and prime, defer the build, state the trigger) · the attractor vocabulary that gives the ladder's rungs their name from the other side · and the held-out empirical check. Not taken, and said so: the paradigm claim (requirements engineering “must die” — here elicitation stays primary and stress discovers what it missed) · tolerances discoverable *only* by simulation · the probability ban outside stressor work · and statistics asserted but not shown in that volume. Adopted at V4.6 as a pass landing at steps 01, 07 and 10 — not an eleventh step, for the same reason delivery was not.
- **Names, scope and change history** — [ANSI/NISO Z39.19-2005 (R2010), *Guidelines for the Construction, Format, and Management of Monolingual Controlled Vocabularies*](https://www.niso.org/publications/ansiniso-z3919-2005-r2010), §§5.3, 6.2–6.6, 7.4–7.8, 8.2–8.4 and 11.2–11.3. Adopted at V4.13 through the [naming discipline](#naming) and steps 01, 03, 04, 09 and 10. The source concerns retrieval; behavioural distinctions, API compatibility and separation of role from implementation are the method's application. Its permission to collapse near-synonyms or broader/narrower terms for retrieval is not imported into executable contracts.
- **The name, once more.** It is Kessler & Sweitzer's, as their own item above says. It also collides with outside-in TDD, which is a different thing under the same two words: there it names the direction tests are written, here it names the direction decisions are derived. The overlap — start at the boundary and let the inside follow — is real, and that is as far as it goes.
